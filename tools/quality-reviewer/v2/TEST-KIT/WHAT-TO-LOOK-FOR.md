# What to look for

Ten minutes per deck. Work through it in order. The checks are ordered so the ones that
would kill the product come first.

---

## 1. Restraint. Count the findings.

**Deep mode allows at most 5.** Count them.

A deck can be bad and still deserve three findings. A reviewer that returns five on every deck
is padding, and padding is the failure mode that makes a lead stop reading. One of your three
decks is genuinely strong. **If that one comes back with five findings and several marked
High impact, the reviewer has failed the most important test in this kit** — it means it
manufactures criticism to fill space, and nothing else it says can be trusted.

- [ ] Findings counted, 5 or fewer
- [ ] On the strongest deck, the review is short and the tone is generous

## 2. The quotes. Spot-check three of them.

Every finding carries a verbatim quote and a slide number. Pick three at random, Ctrl-F them in
the deck file.

**A quote that is not in the deck is the one unrecoverable failure.** A missed problem is a gap;
an invented quote is the tool lying to a student about their own work. Across 40 quotes and four
models this has not happened yet. If it happens to you, stop and tell me immediately.

- [ ] Three quotes checked, found character-for-character
- [ ] Quotes are single items, not two separate lines glued into one sentence

## 3. No grade, on route 1.

The chat path is not supposed to tell you how ready the work is. Search the output for
"Not ready", "Needs substantial", "Needs targeted", "Nearly ready" and for any score out of 5.

The only place those four phrases should appear is the paste-back record at the bottom, where
it lists them as options for **you** to pick from.

- [ ] No readiness level anywhere in the review itself
- [ ] No score out of 5, no rubric numbers
- [ ] It says why it withholds a grade, rather than just omitting it

(On route 2 the desk **does** show a level and a score. That is deliberate: the page cages it
behind a warning strip and a quote gate. Same reviewer, different container.)

## 4. No ghostwriting.

"What to do" should give the direction and the principle. It must never hand over finished
slide text the team can paste.

- [ ] Fixes are directional, not written copy
- [ ] "Answer first" asks the team a question rather than answering it for them

## 5. Does it lead with the point?

Every finding should open with the conclusion, not the background. This is the standard the
tool holds the team to, so it has to meet it.

- [ ] Findings state the problem in the first sentence
- [ ] "Why it costs the client" opens with the consequence, not with context

## 6. The one that actually matters: is it right?

Compare the review against **your own three issues, written before you read it.**

- [ ] Did it find your number one?
- [ ] Did it find something real you had missed?
- [ ] Did it raise anything you would be embarrassed to forward to a team?

The third question is the one to be strict about. A finding that is technically true but that
no lead would act on costs more than it looks, because it teaches the team to skim.

## 7. Would you send it?

The honest test. Not "is it impressive" but: **would you forward this to a student team as it
stands, with your name on it?**

- [ ] Yes, as is
- [ ] Yes, after cutting one or two things
- [ ] No

---

## What is not being tested here

Not the readiness level or the score. Both are known unreliable: the same deck has come back
at two different levels on identical input. That is why the chat path does not produce them.
Do not spend time judging a grade the project already does not trust.

Not speed, and not cost.

## If something breaks

Paste the raw output into the feedback file and say what you expected. A broken run is a better
result than a clean one — every real defect this project has found came from something failing
in front of someone, and every prediction made by reading the prompt instead has been roughly a
coin flip.
