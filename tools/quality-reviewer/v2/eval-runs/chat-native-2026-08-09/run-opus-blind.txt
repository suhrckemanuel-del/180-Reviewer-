Effort to close the findings below: 10 to 16 hours

WHAT WORKS
The deck states the binding financial constraint plainly instead of stepping around it. Slide 3: "Client D has no cash reserves and cannot take on new fixed costs without new income first." That sentence is the most useful one in the deck and the rest of the argument should be built to clear it.

The starting position is quantified rather than described. Slide 2: "Client D covers 60% of its costs from bicycle sales and relies on grants for the rest." A reader knows the size of the problem from one line.

The demand evidence is operational and specific to this client, not a market generality. Slide 4: "Client D turns away repair work it cannot staff." That is the client's own signal and it is worth more than the peer comparison.

WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER

1. The recommendation is the exact thing slide 3 says the client cannot do, and the deck never reconciles the two. [High impact]
   Where: slides 3 and 6
   Quote: "Client D has no cash reserves and cannot take on new fixed costs without new income first." (slide 3)
   Quote: "The board has been clear that hiring is not possible until revenue grows." (slide 3)
   Quote: "Open a retail shopfront within six months and hire three paid repair staff to meet demand." (slide 6)
   Why it costs the client: The client cannot act on this. On one reading the board blocks the lease and the three hires. On the other it signs both inside six months. Same deck, opposite decisions, and nothing in it says which reading governs.
   What to do: Pick one and defend it. Either sequence the plan so income arrives before the fixed costs, with a stated revenue trigger for the shop and for each hire, or argue explicitly why the constraint on slide 3 can be relaxed and what makes that safe. A recommendation has to clear the constraints the deck itself established.
   Answer first: What has to be true, in cash, before Client D can sign a lease and add a salary?

2. The financial case is asserted rather than built: no base, no derivation and nothing measured against the gap the plan exists to close. [High impact]
   Where: slides 5 and 7
   Quote: "Revenue will double in year one." (slide 7)
   Quote: "The shop pays for itself by month nine." (slide 7)
   Quote: "Comparable social enterprises have doubled revenue after opening a shop." (slide 5)
   Why it costs the client: The client is asked to commit a lease and three salaries against figures with no arithmetic behind them. Doubling is not stated against a base, month nine is not stated against the shop's cost, and neither is measured against the 40% of costs grants currently carry.
   What to do: Build the arithmetic and show it. State current revenue, the annual gap the plan must close and the shop's fixed costs including the three roles, then derive the payback from those and show the assumptions. Say where the peer doubling figure comes from and how comparable those peers are in size, city and model, or drop the claim. Every figure the decision leans on needs something it is being measured against.
   Answer first: What must the shop earn each month to cover its own costs plus the grant shortfall, and how does that compare to what the current operation earns?

3. Next steps name three actions and no mechanism, so everything hard about the plan stays on the client's side of the table. [Moderate]
   Where: slides 6 and 9
   Quote: "Sign a lease. Recruit staff. Launch." (slide 9)
   Quote: "Open a retail shopfront within six months and hire three paid repair staff to meet demand." (slide 6)
   Why it costs the client: The client is holding a noun. What the lease costs, who funds the deposit and the first payroll, who owns each step, what releases each hire and what counts as success by when are all still undone, which is the part they needed help with.
   What to do: Carry one step end to end rather than listing three. Cost the lease and the three roles, name an owner and a date for each step and set the revenue trigger that releases each hire. Doing the first step yourselves, a site search with real local rents or a month one cash plan, is what turns advice into something the client can start on.
   Answer first: Who at Client D does step one, in which week, with what money?

4. The deck calls the plan low-risk and states the outcome as certain, while naming no assumption, no risk and no downside. [Moderate]
   Where: slides 6 and 8
   Quote: "The plan is low-risk. Demand is proven and the model is tested by peers." (slide 8)
   Quote: "This will make Client D self-sustaining." (slide 6)
   Why it costs the client: An organisation with no reserves committing to a lease and three salaries is carrying real risk, and certainty removes the client's reason to plan for slower sales. They would find out it was not low-risk only once the fixed costs were signed.
   What to do: State the assumptions the plan rests on and what happens if each fails. Add a downside case, name the early signal that would tell the client it is happening and say what they do then. Replace the certainty with a confidence and the basis for it.
   Answer first: If sales come in at half the forecast by month six, what does the client do, and who decides?

5. The answer to the client's question arrives on slide 6 of 9, and the pages before it are topic labels, so a fast read gives the setup and not the point. [Moderate]
   Where: slides 1, 2, 4, 5 and 6
   Quote: "Client D path to self-sustainability. A two-year plan." (slide 1)
   Quote: "Situation" (slide 2)
   Quote: "The market" (slide 4)
   Quote: "The opportunity" (slide 5)
   Quote: "Open a retail shopfront within six months and hire three paid repair staff to meet demand." (slide 6, the first sentence that states the answer, six pages in)
   Why it costs the client: The reader most likely to skim is the board member deciding on the lease. Five pages in they have a list of topics and no answer, and the recommendation with its reasoning sits past where a fast read stops.
   What to do: Put the answer and the reason for it in one sentence on page one, then let the deck support it. Make each title assert the claim of its page rather than name its subject, so that reading the titles in order reproduces the argument.
   Answer first: If the client read only your titles, what would they conclude, and is that your recommendation?

THE HABIT TO BUILD
Test every recommendation against the constraints you wrote down yourself. The weak habit here is building the case forward from the opportunity and never walking it back through the client's own limits, which is how slide 6 came to recommend what slide 3 rules out. The exercise: before the next draft, put the client's hard constraints on one page (cash, staff, board, timeline) and write beside each recommendation which constraint it has to clear and the evidence that it does. Anything that cannot clear one gets sequenced or dropped.

WHAT THIS REVIEW COULD NOT JUDGE
No evidence or source notes were supplied with the input, so no claim's sourcing could be checked and confidence is Low for that reason.
Whether a retail shopfront is the right route to self-sustainability compared with other options. That turns on domain knowledge this review does not have.
Whether the peer comparison on slide 5 is a fair comparison, since neither the peers nor their conditions are named.
The size of the gap in money, the client's current revenue and the cost of the lease and the three roles. None of it is in the deck.
Whether the board constraint on slide 3 is absolute or negotiable, which changes how finding 1 should be resolved.
The input is text only with no visual layer, so nothing about layout, charts or exhibits was assessed.

QUESTIONS WORTH ASKING THE TEAM
1. What does the 40% grant-funded share cost per year in money, and what must the shop earn to close it?
2. Where do the doubling figure and the month-nine payback come from, and what assumptions sit behind them?
3. Has the board been asked whether the hiring constraint moves for a hire that generates revenue?
4. What does the lease cost, what is the deposit, and where does the cash come from before the shop earns anything?
5. Which peers doubled revenue after opening a shop, and how comparable are they to Client D?
