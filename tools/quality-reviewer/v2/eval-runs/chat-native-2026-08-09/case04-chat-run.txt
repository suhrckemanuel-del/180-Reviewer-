WHAT WORKS
- The deck states its own binding constraint plainly instead of burying it. Slide 3: "Client D
  has no cash reserves and cannot take on new fixed costs without new income first."
- The baseline is specific and quantified early. Slide 2: "Client D covers 60% of its costs
  from bicycle sales and relies on grants for the rest."
- The demand evidence is concrete rather than asserted. Slide 4: "Client D turns away repair
  work it cannot staff."

WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER

1. The recommendation does the one thing the deck says the client cannot do, and never
   reconciles the two. [High impact]
   Where: slides 3 and 6
   Quote: "The board has been clear that hiring is not possible until revenue grows."
   Quote: "Open a retail shopfront within six months and hire three paid repair staff to meet demand."
   Why it costs the client: the client cannot act on this deck. Either the board's condition
   holds and the plan is impossible as written, or it has moved and the deck never says so.
   Those are different decisions, and nothing here lets the client choose between them.
   What to do: state which one is true and build the plan on it. If the condition holds, the
   recommendation has to sequence revenue before fixed cost. A recommendation must survive the
   constraints its own deck asserts.
   Answer first: has the board's position changed, or does the plan assume it will change?

2. The doubling claim carries the whole financial case and is never tested against what
   self-sustainability actually costs. [Moderate]
   Where: slides 5 and 7
   Quote: "Comparable social enterprises have doubled revenue after opening a shop."
   Quote: "Revenue will double in year one. The shop pays for itself by month nine."
   Why it costs the client: doubling revenue only matters if it covers the 40% gap plus three
   salaries plus a lease. The deck never puts those two numbers next to each other, so the
   client cannot tell whether the plan reaches self-sustainability or falls short of it.
   What to do: name what self-sustaining costs per year with the new fixed costs included, then
   show the revenue path against that number. A figure with nothing to be measured against
   describes rather than analyses.
   Answer first: what does Client D need to earn to cover its costs once the shop and three
   staff are in place?

3. The deck calls the plan low-risk while its own pages describe the conditions that make it
   risky. [Moderate]
   Where: slide 8
   Quote: "The plan is low-risk. Demand is proven and the model is tested by peers."
   Why it costs the client: a client reading "low-risk" will not stress-test the downside. The
   deck has already said there are no reserves and no room for fixed costs, which is the
   definition of a plan with no margin for error.
   What to do: replace the claim with the actual risk position and what would have to be true
   for it to hold. Confidence should match the evidence behind it, not the strength of the
   recommendation.
   Answer first: what happens to Client D if revenue grows at half the assumed rate?

4. The next steps name activities, not a plan anyone can be held to. [Moderate]
   Where: slide 9
   Quote: "Sign a lease. Recruit staff. Launch."
   Why it costs the client: nobody owns anything, nothing has a date or a cost, and the lease
   is unfunded on the deck's own account of the finances. A client cannot start on this, which
   for a final deliverable is where the value was supposed to be.
   What to do: give each step an owner, a date, a cost and the measure that says it worked, and
   say how the lease is financed before revenue arrives. A recommendation without a first step
   the client can take is an opinion.
   Answer first: who signs the lease, with what money, and by when?

THE HABIT TO BUILD
Check your recommendation against the constraints your own deck asserts, before you write it up.
This deck's situation section and its recommendation section were each written well and written
separately, which is the habit worth breaking. Exercise: before finalising, list every constraint
your own pages state, then write one line per constraint saying how the recommendation survives
it. Anything you cannot answer is either a change to the recommendation or a question for the
client.

WHAT THIS REVIEW COULD NOT JUDGE
- Whether the peer benchmark is real or comparable. No source is given in the deck.
- The financial model behind the doubling claim. It is not in the deliverable.
- Whether the board's hiring position has changed since it was stated.
- Anything visual. This was reviewed as text, so layout, design and slide density are unassessed.

QUESTIONS WORTH ASKING THE TEAM
- What does self-sustaining cost per year, once the shopfront and three salaries are included?
- Which comparable enterprises, at what starting revenue, and in what market?
- Has the board's condition on hiring changed, or does the plan depend on it changing?
- How is the lease financed before the new revenue arrives?

HOW TO CHECK THIS FEEDBACK

quotes in your own work, that point is wrong. Drop it and say so. This review advises. A human
decides, and nothing here goes to your client on its own.
