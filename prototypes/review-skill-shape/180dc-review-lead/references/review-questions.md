# The five review questions

Ask each question of the whole deliverable. Each one lists what experienced 180DC leads look for.
Most candidate findings die at step 5; collect generously here.

## 1. Decision: does the client know what to do next?

- **Named, not shown.** The most common complaint about competent student work. The deck says
  "build a mailing list" or "find a partner" and moves on, leaving the client holding a noun.
  Leads ask for, in rising order: the mechanism, one worked case end to end, the arithmetic with
  its assumptions visible, the first step done. This is a decision finding, not polish.
- **Why without how.** Pages on why a recommendation is good, none on how it is done.
- **Could the client have produced this themselves, faster?** Compiled, restated, well-organised
  information the client already had. Signals: the closing section names no decision; landscape
  pages that never say what they mean for this client; brochure prose true of any organisation.
  When sourcing cannot be traced, say it should be verified. Do not suggest it was invented.

## 2. Framing: is it the right problem, bounded?

- Is the problem specific, the scope bounded, the success criteria stated?
- Does the deck drift between problems, or answer a different question than the brief? Raise the
  drift as a finding. Do not decide the new question was the better one.

## 3. Storyline: does the message survive one fast read?

**The reader test.** Read the summary, the titles and any recommendation table first, then follow
references into the body. Say what the client can already understand before proposing anything.
Raise a communication finding only when you can name the specific choice, tradeoff or next step
the reader cannot recover. Common shapes:

- No governing summary: no page that states the answer and its reason first. A summary table
  with nearby rationale counts as one.
- Volume against signal: everything the team learned, not what the reader needs to decide.
- Order that does not follow the argument; related material split across distant sections.
- Analysis and recommendation mixed, so the reader cannot tell today from the proposal.

Density alone is not a finding. Without page images, do not judge visual layout from text.

## 4. Evidence: can it carry the weight put on it?

- Evidence is judged against the **specific conclusion** it carries. Ten interviews can be strong
  for what people care about and far too thin for a price point.
- Effort often sits where the team enjoyed working, not where the decision sits.
- The outside view (market, competitors, what to learn from them) is usually the thinnest part.
- A number that drives a recommendation with no reasoning behind it is a finding.

**The comparator test.** A number means nothing alone. For each figure the recommendation leans
on, ask: compared to what? So is that good or bad? If bad, a symptom of what? Raise this **once**
for the deliverable, naming the figures it covers. Check exhibits and appendix first. Name the
missing comparator; never invent one.

## 5. Recommendation: can it be carried out?

- Does each recommendation have an owner, a timeframe, a measure and a first step?
- Is it feasible for this client's size and budget?
- **Should-we choices** (choose or fund a course of action): ask the team to compare options on
  MEASURE / COST / COVERAGE / RECOMMENDATION and say what would prove the choice wrong in a year.
  Ask them to do it; do not fill it in. For other deliverables, do not force the framework.

## Principles you may name

Four, and only these on the Framework line: the Minto pyramid (answer first, then support it; a
practitioner standard), the reader test (question 3), the comparator test (question 4) and
MEASURE / COST / COVERAGE (question 5). Name one only when it explains the finding, and say why
in the deck's own terms. Two more ideas guide the review without being named: evidence must carry
the decision's weight, and what is unknown is not the same as what is absent.
