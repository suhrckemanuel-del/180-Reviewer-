# The checks, for one slide with one chart

<!-- PROTOTYPE. In the build, "The title is the so-what", "Compared to what?" and "The ghost
deck" come from shared/principles.md; "The numbers match" and "One message" are this skill's. -->

Each check gives what a break looks like, what isn't one, what to tell them and a test they can
run. The direction names the change; it never contains the words.

## 1. The title is the so-what

A title states what the slide proves, for this client. "Missed pickups by month" is a label; a
full sentence someone could disagree with is a so-what.

**A break:** a title that names the chart's topic; a title true of any chart ("Retention varies
by format"); the takeaway in a subtitle while the title stays a label.

**Not a break:** a takeaway you'd phrase differently; a title that's long but says one thing.

**Tell them:** what the numbers table shows that the title doesn't use (the biggest change, the
gap, the group that didn't move), and ask what that means for the client. **Check it:** could
someone disagree with the title? Does the slide still say it if the chart is deleted?

## 2. The numbers match

Every figure in the title, and on the slide, is one the data produces, at the rounding shown,
for the group and period the sentence names.

**A break:** a number the data doesn't produce; the right number on the wrong base (a drop for
online bookings sold as the drop for all bookings; a share of respondents sold as a share of
all clients); "doubled", "halved", "a third" when the ratio isn't close (say what it is:
1.6 times is not double); a rounding that flips the meaning (49.6% as "half").

**Not a break:** rounding to whole numbers when the table has decimals; "about" with a figure
inside a few percent.

**Tell them:** the number, what the data says, and which rows produced each. Never supply a
replacement sentence. **Check it:** for each number in the title, point to the row in the table.

## 3. Compared to what?

A number alone doesn't say whether it's good or bad. The chart has to show what the title
compares against: before and after, a target, last year, a peer, a group without the change.

**A break:** a title claiming a change while the chart shows only the end state; a claim about
an effect with no group that didn't get it, when the data has one; a percentage with no base
("70% of what?"); no source line.

**Also a break:** a title claiming a cause ("buddy onboarding doubles retention", "reminders
cut no-shows") from groups that chose themselves, when the data can't rule out another
difference between them. Say how the groups were formed, or that the data doesn't say; a
before-and-after with an untouched comparison group is the strongest the data usually gets.

**Not a break:** a slide that only describes a state, with its source; a comparison in the
caption when the chart can't hold it.

**Tell them:** the comparison the data allows that the chart or title leaves out. **Check it:**
cover everything but the number. Can a reader say whether it's good or bad?

## 4. One message

One slide proves one thing. **A break:** a title joining two claims the chart can't both show;
two charts on one slide; a chart showing six series when the title is about one. **Tell them:**
which claim this chart proves, and that the other gets its own slide.

## 5. The ghost deck (3 or more slides)

Read the titles alone, in order. They should tell the story from the answer down to the next
step. **A break:** a title that doesn't follow from the one before; two titles saying the same
thing; titles ending without the answer or a next step. **Tell them:** where the story breaks,
by slide number.

## Not your job here

Layout of their whole deck, fonts, their template, grammar, whether the client's data is right.
Say once, if relevant, what you couldn't judge.
