WHAT WORKS

The recommendation answers the question directly and with a deliberately conservative approach. "Client E should launch a paid membership scheme, starting with a single tier in the autumn." The board gets a clear yes with a bounded scope.

The phased implementation is honest about uncertainty. "Month one: soft launch to 200 contacts. Month two: review conversion against the 8% target. Month three: full launch if the soft launch clears 5%." This is testable and does not commit the coordinator to a binary bet.

The evidence sits where it should. Income dependency on grants is sourced to 2024 accounts; the engaged base is documented in attendance data; comparables come from real organisations' public websites. Nothing is invented, and the team knew which data points belonged to which claim.

WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER

1. The 8% conversion assumption is called out as the main thing to test, but the reasoning behind it is incomplete and creates reading discomfort for a board. [High impact]
   Where: Slide 8, Slide 9
   Quote: "The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test."
   Why it costs the client: The board cannot evaluate whether to trust this number without understanding the thinking. Saying it is "conservative" against attendance is not a bridge—people who attend free events do not automatically convert to paying members at any rate. Without a credible derivation, the board either has to push back or commit to a soft launch they have not fully validated. It creates a hidden dependency that the soft launch becomes a decision gate the team does not yet know how to operate.
   What to do: Show the reasoning for 8%. Is it conservative against comparable organisations' conversion rates? Against the client's own upgrade rates in other income streams (if any)? Against what the team would need to make the maths work? Name the comparison, not just the direction. The board needs to know what assumption they are actually backing.
   Answer first: What evidence or benchmark supports the choice of 8% over, say, 4% or 12%?

2. The soft launch has a success ceiling but no path if it falls short of the 5% target. [High impact]
   Where: Slide 10
   Quote: "Month three: full launch if the soft launch clears 5%."
   Why it costs the client: The coordinator is left holding an ambiguous hand-off. If the soft launch hits 3%, is the recommendation killed, shelved, iterated, or retargeted? This is not a polish note. The board is committing staff time and deciding whether to build the operational machinery (partner discount negotiations, event planning, database work). They need to know what "no go" looks like and what happens to that work if the test fails.
   What to do: Spell out the decision rules. If soft launch hits 5–8%, full launch proceeds. If it hits 3–5%, go to [one of: a second wave to the next 300 contacts, a pricing test, a benefits redesign, a delay]. If it hits below 3%, [one of: pause membership, pursue another diversification path, revisit in 12 months]. The point is not to predict the future; it is to give the coordinator a rulebook so she is not improvising at the crux.
   Answer first: What would the team recommend the coordinator do if the soft launch converts at 3% instead of 8%?

3. The value proposition for Client E's members is generic and does not show how it connects to this client's actual practice. [Moderate]
   Where: Slide 7
   Quote: "Priority booking, two members-only events a year and a discount with local partners. These use capacity Client E already has."
   Why it costs the client: The board reads this and can picture priority booking and events, but cannot tell whether this offer is tailored to what Client E's actual members care about or whether it is a checkbox list. The phrase "These use capacity Client E already has" is defensive and does not answer the question a paying member asks: why would I pay? The board might not catch it, but it sits under the 8% assumption. If the value prop is thin, 8% is optimistic.
   What to do: Ground the benefits in what Client E's own members actually do and care about. If 32% of the mailing list attends events, what do they cite when they book? What do local partners report about why members visit them? Do member interviews or even quick polling of the mailing list. One concrete example: "Priority booking for the autumn festival reduced no-shows by 40% in comparable organisations and is worth £25 in perceived value per member"—something from data or lived testimony. You are not inventing; you are proving the offer lands.
   Answer first: What benefits did Client E's own audience say they would value in a membership tier?

WHAT THIS REVIEW COULD NOT JUDGE

Whether local partners will accept a discount arrangement with the volume and timing Client E expects.
Whether the coordinator's available hours are realistic for soft launch execution and tracking.
Whether 45 pounds is the right price point for this specific client's market; pricing studies on the three comparables are not in the document, and local arts sector pricing can vary sharply by reputation, city, and offering.
Whether two members-only events per year is feasible to add to the existing calendar without strain.
Whether the mailing list is clean enough and engaged enough that 8% of it is a credible floor.

THE HABIT TO BUILD

The weak habit: treating an assumption as solid because it is conservative. The pattern: "Our figure is conservative" becomes a shield instead of a bridge. Conservatism is a direction, not a justification. The exercise: for the next recommendation, pick the number you are least sure of. Write down what evidence would move it up or down, in concrete terms. Share that page with the lead before you put the number in the deck. Make conservatism auditable, not assumed.

QUESTIONS WORTH ASKING THE TEAM

1. What does 8% actually track to? Are you saying 8% of mailing list subscribers will sign up, or 8% of people you contact will convert, or 8% of year-one revenue will come through membership? The phrasing shifted between slides.

2. If the soft launch to 200 contacts returns 3–4% conversion, what would you recommend to the board in month two: revise the offer, retarget the full launch, or pause and iterate? Say it now so the coordinator knows.

3. Have you or the client tested whether the local partners are willing to participate? A discount programme only works if partners agree and track uptake.

4. What is the monthly or seasonal shape of the coordinator's work? If month one is the soft launch, what else lands on that schedule, and does the launch have breathing room?

5. Does the mailing list have a creation date and update cycle? The board will want to know how fresh it is.
