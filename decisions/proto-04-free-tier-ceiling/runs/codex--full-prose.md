WHAT WORKS

- The deck answers the client’s question directly and chooses a cautious route. Slide 2: "Our answer is yes, with a deliberately small start."
- The case for diversifying income starts from relevant client data. Slide 3: "Project grants are 80% of Client E's income (2024 accounts) and each is single-year."
- Implementation ownership is explicit. Slide 10: "The Client E coordinator owns the launch."

WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER

1. The scale decision uses two conversion thresholds without explaining how they lead to different actions. [High impact]
   Where: Slide 10
   Quote: "The Client E coordinator owns the launch. Month one: soft launch to 200 contacts. Month two: review conversion against the 8% target. Month three: full launch if the soft launch clears 5%."
   Why it costs the client: The board cannot tell whether a 6% result is a failed pilot or sufficient evidence to scale. That ambiguity affects the actual launch decision, not just how the recommendation is presented.
   What to do: Define the target, minimum viable threshold and stop condition as distinct decision gates. Ground each gate in the income case and capacity constraint.
   Answer first: At what result should Client E stop, adjust or scale, and what evidence justifies each choice?

2. The governing rationale arrives on Slide 6, so a fast reader receives the action before the reason for choosing this particular model. [Moderate]
   Where: Slides 1 and 6
   Quote: "Client E should launch a paid membership scheme, starting with a single tier in the autumn." "Launching one tier at 45 pounds, rather than three tiers at once, keeps admin low and lets Client E learn before it scales."
   Why it costs the client: The board may remember a generic instruction to launch membership but miss that the recommendation is specifically designed around limited capacity and uncertainty. That distinction is central to evaluating the proposal.
   What to do: Bring the action and its governing reason together at the start. Preserve the later evidence, but make the capacity and learning logic clear before the supporting sections.
   Answer first: What is the single reason this launch design is right for Client E rather than merely common practice?

3. The conversion and revenue case does not give the board a decision-grade comparator. [High impact]
   Where: Slide 8
   Quote: "If 8% of the mailing list joins at 45 pounds, that is about 5,000 pounds in year one. The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test."
   Why it costs the client: Attendance is not equivalent to willingness to buy membership, while 5,000 pounds is not compared with the funding gap, current income or delivery cost. The board cannot judge plausibility or materiality.
   What to do: Show a sensitivity range rather than relying on one assumption. Seek a relevant conversion benchmark, state the remaining uncertainty and compare net proceeds with a defined diversification need or target.
   Answer first: What minimum net contribution would make membership worthwhile, and what evidence supports the conversion range used to test it?

4. The recommendation assumes existing capacity rather than showing that the full scheme fits one part-time role. [High impact]
   Where: Slides 7 and 9
   Quote: "These use capacity Client E already has." "We assume the mailing list is accurate and that members-only events do not strain staff."
   Why it costs the client: The board could commit to benefits that displace programme delivery or create unfunded workload. A 200-contact pilot may test launch effort without proving that administration and events remain manageable at full scale.
   What to do: Estimate setup and recurring hours by activity, identify capacity-limited benefits and test workload at both pilot and full-launch scale. Add an operational gate alongside the conversion gate.
   Answer first: Which existing activities would absorb the work, and what workload result would prevent a full launch?

THE HABIT TO BUILD

Turn every important assumption into a decision rule. The weak habit is naming an uncertainty without connecting it to what the client should do when the result is above or below expectations. As an exercise, create a four-column assumption register: assumption, evidence or comparator, test threshold and resulting action.

WHAT THIS REVIEW COULD NOT JUDGE

The accuracy of the accounts, box-office data, funder letters or comparable pricing was not independently verified.
The visual hierarchy, chart legibility and full-slide reading experience were not assessed because only extracted text was provided.
Whether 45 pounds is the optimal price could not be determined from three stated comparables alone.
The actual staff time required for administration, member events and partner discounts was not provided.
The reliability and consent status of the mailing list could not be assessed.
The board’s risk appetite and required income-diversification target were not provided.
No conclusion was reached on whether the proposed benefits are attractive enough to drive conversion.

QUESTIONS WORTH ASKING THE TEAM

1. Why is 5% sufficient to trigger full launch when 8% is the stated target?
2. What relevant membership, donation or campaign conversion evidence could replace the attendance-rate comparison?
3. What net income remains after staff time, events, discounts and other delivery costs?
4. How many coordinator hours would the pilot and full scheme require?
5. What result would cause the team to recommend stopping or redesigning the scheme?
