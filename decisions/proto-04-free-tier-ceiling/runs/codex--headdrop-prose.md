WHAT WORKS

- The recommendation is unusually clear and appears at the beginning: “Client E should launch a paid membership scheme, starting with a single tier in the autumn.” (Slide 1)
- The case for considering diversification starts from specific organisational evidence: “Project grants are 80% of Client E's income (2024 accounts) and each is single-year.” (Slide 3)
- The proposed operating model shows useful restraint: “Launching one tier at 45 pounds, rather than three tiers at once, keeps admin low and lets Client E learn before it scales.” (Slide 6)

WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER

1. The central 8% conversion assumption is not supported by evidence that can carry the recommendation. [High impact]  
   Where: Slide 8  
   Quote: “The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test.”  
   Why it costs the client: Attendance and paid conversion are different behaviours. Without a bridge between them, the board cannot tell whether £5,000 is a plausible base case, an optimistic scenario, or simply an arithmetic illustration.  
   What to do: Treat 8% explicitly as a hypothesis. Show a conversion range, the resulting income at each point, and any available evidence connecting attendance, engagement and willingness to pay.  
   Answer first: What observed behaviour makes a mailing-list contact likely to pay £45, rather than merely attend an event?

2. The soft-launch design does not yet provide a reliable decision rule for launching to the full list. [High impact]  
   Where: Slides 9–10  
   Quote: “A soft launch to the most engaged 200 contacts tests both before the full launch.”  
   Why it costs the client: The most engaged contacts are likely to convert better than the remaining list, while the deck uses different thresholds for review and progression. A successful pilot could therefore create false confidence about full-list performance.  
   What to do: Define the pilot population, success metric, minimum threshold and subsequent forecast as one coherent rule. Adjust explicitly for the pilot group’s unusually high engagement and specify what happens below, near and above the threshold.  
   Answer first: What soft-launch result would imply that the broader mailing list can convert at a sustainable rate?

3. The deck shows gross revenue but not whether membership would materially diversify income or generate worthwhile net income. [High impact]  
   Where: Slide 8  
   Quote: “If 8% of the mailing list joins at 45 pounds, that is about 5,000 pounds in year one.”  
   Why it costs the client: The board cannot judge the scheme’s importance without seeing £5,000 against total income, non-renewing grants and delivery costs. Gross revenue may overstate the scheme’s contribution once events, discounts, payments and staff time are included.  
   What to do: Add a compact sensitivity case showing gross income, plausible direct costs, staff time and net contribution at several conversion rates. Compare the result with total income and the funding gap membership is intended to address.  
   Answer first: How much unrestricted net income must the scheme produce to justify the coordinator’s time?

4. Existing staff capacity is asserted rather than demonstrated. [High impact]  
   Where: Slide 7  
   Quote: “These use capacity Client E already has.”  
   Why it costs the client: With one part-time coordinator and no expansion budget, fulfilment is central to feasibility. The deck does not quantify launch administration, member communication, partner management, event delivery, payment handling or renewal work.  
   What to do: Map the recurring and one-off tasks, estimate hours by month, identify peak periods and state what existing activity would be displaced. Test capacity separately from conversion during the pilot.  
   Answer first: How many coordinator hours will the scheme require during launch and in a normal month?

5. The member offer is not connected to evidence about what the target audience values. [Moderate]  
   Where: Slide 7  
   Quote: “Priority booking, two members-only events a year and a discount with local partners.”  
   Why it costs the client: Price comparisons show what other organisations charge, but not whether Client E’s contacts value these benefits enough to pay. A weak offer would undermine both the conversion forecast and the interpretation of the pilot.  
   What to do: Show how the benefits were selected, distinguish low-cost from capacity-intensive benefits, and obtain lightweight evidence of willingness to pay before fixing the offer.  
   Answer first: Which benefit is expected to drive purchase, and what evidence supports that expectation?

6. The risk assessment is too narrow for a board decision. [Moderate]  
   Where: Slide 9  
   Quote: “The main risk is low conversion.”  
   Why it costs the client: Low conversion matters, but the board also needs visibility on delivery burden, benefit costs, partner dependency, renewals and possible displacement of existing donations or ticket income. These risks could change the economics even if recruitment succeeds.  
   What to do: Prioritise a short set of decision-relevant risks by likelihood and consequence. Give each an early indicator, mitigation and owner, separating risks the pilot can test from those it cannot.  
   Answer first: What could make the scheme unattractive even if at least 8% of the pilot group joins?

THE HABIT TO BUILD

The weak habit is moving from a plausible idea directly to a single-point recommendation. Build the habit of exposing the chain from evidence to assumption to economics to decision. As an exercise, take the 8% figure and produce a one-page assumption map: supporting evidence, contrary evidence, sensitivity range, test method and the decision that follows from each outcome.

WHAT THIS REVIEW COULD NOT JUDGE

- Whether paid membership is better than other income-diversification options; no alternative routes are compared.
- Whether £45 is the correct price; the comparator range is reported, but its underlying comparability could not be verified.
- Whether the mailing-list and attendance measures refer to comparable people and periods.
- Whether the benefits can genuinely be delivered from spare capacity.
- Whether the source figures and comparator websites were transcribed accurately.
- Whether tax, payment, data-protection or contractual considerations apply.
- The deck’s visual clarity, because only extracted text was provided.
- Any evidence contained in appendices, speaker notes or supporting files not included here.

QUESTIONS WORTH ASKING THE TEAM

1. What evidence, beyond attendance, supports a paid conversion rate of 8%?
2. Why does the pilot review against 8% but permit a full launch above 5%?
3. How does the forecast change when the pilot group’s higher engagement is accounted for?
4. What is the expected net contribution after delivery costs and coordinator time?
5. Which important risks cannot be tested through a 200-contact soft launch?
