WHAT WORKS

WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER

1. The connection between 32% attendance and 8% membership conversion is asserted, not shown. [High impact]
   Where: Slide 8
   Quote: "The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test."
   Why it costs the client: The board will ask directly: how does an event attendance rate become a membership rate? The team says it is conservative but offers no reasoning for why attending a show means someone will pay to join. The gap between the two behaviors is real, and the team has not crossed it. Without that bridge, the board doubts the whole plan.
   What to do: Work backwards from the 8%. Is it based on conversation with members? Comparable non-profit conversion rates? A subset of the 32% who attend repeatedly? Show one of these. Even rough (e.g., 65% of attendees are repeats; of those, we estimate 12% would join based on conversations) makes the claim land.
   Answer first: Of the 1,400 on the mailing list, how many have actually attended an event, and how many have Client E staff spoken to about membership interest?

2. The soft launch succeeds or fails at a target that is not the same as the main assumption. [High impact]
   Where: Slide 8 and Slide 10
   Quote from Slide 8: "The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test."
   Quote from Slide 10: "Month three: full launch if the soft launch clears 5%."
   Why it costs the client: The team says 8% is the main assumption. But the soft launch will clear 5% and proceed anyway. The coordinator will hit Month Two confused about what success means. These thresholds are a contradiction the board will catch.
   What to do: State the decision rule explicitly. Either 5% is the true go/no-go threshold and 8% is what we hope for, or 8% is the actual minimum and the soft launch is structured differently. Pick one and defend it in Slide 10.
   Answer first: What is the financial or operational consequence of launching to the full base at a 5% conversion rate?

3. The belief that members-only events fit inside the coordinator's capacity is named as an assumption to test, not a fact to bank on. [High impact]
   Where: Slide 7 and Slide 9
   Quote from Slide 7: "These use capacity Client E already has."
   Quote from Slide 9: "We assume the mailing list is accurate and that members-only events do not strain staff."
   Why it costs the client: Slide 7 presents two members-only events a year as part of the offer, built in. Slide 9 names it as an assumption to test. Those say opposite things. Client E has one part-time coordinator and grant deadlines and event production. The board needs to know whether this is a given or a risk the team has named but not solved.
   What to do: Either confirm the capacity exists (show the math: coordinator hours today, coordinator hours after launch, difference), or name the unbudgeted load honestly and say whether the team thinks the board should hire someone for it.
   Answer first: What is the month-by-month time commitment from the coordinator for months 1–3, and where does it fit in a normal week?

4. The mailing list quality assumption is stated but never defended. [Moderate]
   Where: Slide 9
   Quote: "We assume the mailing list is accurate and that members-only events do not strain staff."
   Why it costs the client: A mailing list is only as good as its last clean-up. If 30% of the list is dead email or people who unsubscribed long ago, the soft launch to 200 selected contacts might actually reach 140 active people, changing the math. The team has named this risk but given the coordinator no way to check before Month One starts.
   What to do: Recommend one concrete step in the soft launch plan: run a small test send to 20 addresses and measure the bounce rate. If it is above X%, pause and clean the list first.
   Answer first: When was the mailing list last cleaned, and does anyone on Client E's team actively manage it?

THE HABIT TO BUILD

The team has done strong evidence work and built a sensible recommendation. The habit to build is asking "what is the gap between my evidence and my claim?" at every turn. The 32% attendance rate is real. The 8% conversion is an assumption. Naming that gap (and bridging it with either data or reasoning) is what makes a recommendation a client can trust. The team named it once (Slide 8: "is the main thing to test"). Carry that habit all the way through, especially at the moment your claim changes shape.

WHAT THIS REVIEW COULD NOT JUDGE

Whether the pricing point of 45 pounds is optimal for Client E's specific audience or local market context.
Whether local partner discounts have been informally confirmed or are fully speculative at this stage.
Whether comparable non-profits' membership schemes are truly comparable (e.g., do they also run on part-time staff? Do they have different income baselines?).
Whether the audience for members-only events in an arts non-profit at this scale is large enough to support two events a year without diluting quality.

QUESTIONS WORTH ASKING THE TEAM

1. Have you spoken directly to 20–30 engaged attendees about whether they would pay 45 pounds to join, and what did you hear?
2. At a 5% conversion rate on the soft launch, what is the annual membership revenue, and is that enough to matter given Client E's grants risk?
3. If the soft launch converts at 5% and the team recommends proceeding, how do you explain that to the board when the stated assumption was 8%?
4. Has the part-time coordinator reviewed the Month 1–3 plan and signed off that it fits her actual hours?
5. What is the bounce rate on the mailing list, and when was it last validated?
