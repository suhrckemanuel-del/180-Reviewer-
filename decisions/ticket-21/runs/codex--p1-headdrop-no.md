WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER

1. The 8% conversion assumption is not supported by the attendance rate used to justify it. [High impact]  
   Where: Slide 8  
   Quote: "The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test."  
   Why it costs the client: Event attendance and willingness to buy an annual membership are different behaviours. Without evidence connecting them, the board may treat a labelled “conservative” case as a forecast and approve the scheme on confidence the analysis has not earned.  
   What to do: Present 8% explicitly as an unvalidated scenario, not a defensible base case. Show a range of conversion outcomes and identify any relevant behavioural evidence that could narrow it. If none exists, make validation part of the decision.  
   Answer first: What observed behaviour makes an attendee or mailing-list contact likely to pay for membership?

2. The financial case shows gross income but not whether that income is material or what the scheme contributes after delivery costs. [High impact]  
   Where: Slide 8  
   Quote: "If 8% of the mailing list joins at 45 pounds, that is about 5,000 pounds in year one."  
   Why it costs the client: The board cannot tell whether approximately £5,000 meaningfully reduces grant dependence, replaces any part of the non-renewing income or remains worthwhile after events, administration and member-benefit costs. The arithmetic is correct, but the decision economics are incomplete.  
   What to do: Compare gross membership income with direct costs, staff time and the income gap the scheme is intended to address. Show the contribution under several conversion cases and state whether “year one” means the first twelve months or the remaining months after an autumn launch.  
   Answer first: What minimum net contribution would make the scheme worth the coordinator’s limited capacity?

3. Comparator pricing does not yet establish that £45 is the right price for Client E’s offer. [Moderate]  
   Where: Slide 5  
   Quote: "Three comparable local arts non-profits run membership at 40 to 60 pounds a year (their public websites, 2025)."  
   Why it costs the client: The range shows that local membership schemes exist, but not that the organisations, audiences and benefits are sufficiently comparable. The board cannot see whether £45 reflects Client E’s proposed value or was selected mainly because it lies inside the observed range.  
   What to do: Compare the three schemes on benefits, audience, organisational scale and any other visible terms that affect price. Then connect the proposed price to Client E’s offer and treat willingness to pay as something the soft launch must test.  
   Answer first: What makes each organisation genuinely comparable to Client E rather than merely nearby and arts-focused?

4. The claim that the benefits use existing capacity is asserted rather than demonstrated. [High impact]  
   Where: Slide 7  
   Quote: "These use capacity Client E already has."  
   Why it costs the client: With one part-time coordinator and no budget for new staff, capacity is a central feasibility condition. A 200-person launch may test enrolment administration, but it will not by itself demonstrate the workload of two events, partner management and ongoing member service.  
   What to do: Map each promised benefit to tasks, timing, staff hours and any external dependency. Set an operational cap or fallback for benefits that become burdensome, and distinguish what the pilot can test immediately from what remains an annual delivery assumption.  
   Answer first: How many coordinator hours will the scheme require at launch, each month and around the two member events?

5. The pilot has two different conversion thresholds and uses a selected audience without explaining how its result supports a full launch. [High impact]  
   Where: Slide 10  
   Quote: "Month two: review conversion against the 8% target. Month three: full launch if the soft launch clears 5%."  
   Why it costs the client: The board cannot tell why 5% permits expansion when 8% is the stated target. Because the pilot uses the most engaged contacts, its conversion rate may also overstate what the remaining mailing list will achieve, creating a false-positive launch decision.  
   What to do: Define one decision rule with a rationale, the outcome measured and the action attached to each result. Account explicitly for the pilot group’s selection bias, and include workload and member response—not conversion alone—in the expansion decision.  
   Answer first: What evidence would make the board launch, revise or stop after the pilot, and why?

THE HABIT TO BUILD

Build recommendations around decision-grade assumptions rather than single-point estimates. The weak habit here is acknowledging uncertainty but still allowing one unsupported number to carry the recommendation. As an exercise, create an assumption table with five columns: assumption, current evidence, plausible range, test method and decision consequence. Every number that changes the recommendation should appear in it.

WHAT THIS REVIEW COULD NOT JUDGE

- Whether the accounts, box-office data and funder information are accurate or complete.
- Whether the three comparator organisations and their offers are genuinely comparable.
- Whether mailing-list contacts have previously expressed interest in membership.
- Whether the proposed benefits are attractive enough to support the £45 price.
- The coordinator’s actual available hours and the operational burden of the scheme.
- The value of the two grants that are not expected to renew.
- Whether unseen speaker notes, charts or appendices contain supporting analysis.
- Whether membership is the best diversification route compared with alternatives not assessed in this deck.

QUESTIONS WORTH ASKING THE TEAM

1. How exactly is the 32% event-attendance rate defined, and how much of that attendance comes from repeat attendees?
2. What happens to net contribution at lower, central and higher conversion outcomes?
3. What evidence links the proposed benefits and £45 price to Client E’s audience?
4. Why is 8% the target but 5% sufficient for a full launch?
5. Which parts of workload can the 200-contact pilot genuinely test before the board commits?
