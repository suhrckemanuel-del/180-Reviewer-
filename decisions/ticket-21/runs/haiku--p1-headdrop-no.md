WHAT WORKS

The recommendation moves from diagnosis to action. "Client E should launch a paid membership scheme, starting with a single tier in the autumn" is specific enough that the board could greenlight it on Monday. The soft-launch gate—testing against a real threshold before full commitment—is exactly what an under-resourced nonprofit needs: permission to learn cheaply.

The case for urgency is clear and sourced. "Two of five grants are not expected to renew, per the funder letters on file" is documentary, not speculative. The mailing list already exists ("1,400 people on its mailing list and a 32% event-attendance rate") and that is a genuine asset.

The implementation plan names an owner and a timeline. "The Client E coordinator owns the launch" with month-by-month milestones is what a nonprofit board needs to hand back to staff on Tuesday.

WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER

1. The 8% conversion assumption needs one more step of reasoning to defend it in front of the board. [High impact]
   Where: Slide 8
   Quote: "The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test."
   Why it costs the client: The board is being asked to approve a membership launch whose entire revenue case rests on 8% conversion. You identify it as the key assumption, which is honest. But the connection—why a 32% event-attendance rate predicts 8% membership conversion—is not explained. The board cannot evaluate the assumption you are asking them to accept.
   What to do: Show the math. What has a comparable nonprofit actually achieved? If this data doesn't exist, say so and show instead how you arrived at 8%—did a sample of engaged contacts indicate interest? Did a peer organization tell you their rate? The principle: an assumption that drives the whole case needs to be shown, not just flagged.
   Answer first: Where did 8% come from, and what would justify it to someone skeptical?

2. The claim that member management uses "capacity Client E already has" contradicts the known constraint of one part-time coordinator. [Moderate impact]
   Where: Slide 7 and the Known Constraints
   Quote: "These use capacity Client E already has" and "One part-time coordinator. No budget for new staff."
   Why it costs the client: A part-time coordinator managing one organization cannot absorb member onboarding, event planning for member-only sessions, and partner relations without something breaking. The soft launch tests demand—good—but only on a subset. The full launch assumes the same coordinator scales to 112+ members (8% of 1,400). The honest version acknowledges what has to give.
   What to do: Name what the coordinator will drop or what assumption is wrong. Either the number of members will stay small enough one person can manage it (and reframe the revenue expectation), or the timeline to hire support or automate is part of the plan, or the launch is conditional on another constraint changing.
   Answer first: What will the coordinator actually have time for in month three?

3. The soft-launch conversion gate (5%) is lower than the business-case assumption (8%), creating ambiguity in the go/no-go decision. [Moderate impact]
   Where: Slide 10
   Quote: "Month two: review conversion against the 8% target. Month three: full launch if the soft launch clears 5%."
   Why it costs the client: If 8% is what sustains the revenue story, why do you launch at 5%? Is 5% viable, or are you launching a program you don't believe will work? The gap signals either that 8% is not defensible (in which case the revenue case is weaker) or that the launch gate is wrong (in which case 5% launches a losing product).
   What to do: Choose one. Either the soft launch must hit 8% to justify the full launch and the revenue model holds, or articulate why 5% is enough and revise the revenue case. The logic should be transparent enough that when the soft launch hits 6% the coordinator knows what to do.
   Answer first: Is 5% a success or a fallback?

WHAT THE HABIT TO BUILD

The habit is flagging your own main assumption and then stopping. You do it excellently—"the main thing to test." The next step is explaining why you believed it in the first place. When you tell a client "this is the risk," the kindest thing you can do is also show them why you thought the number was reasonable. It takes 30 seconds and saves hours of post-presentation questions.

WHAT THIS REVIEW COULD NOT JUDGE

- Whether 8% or 5% is realistic for a UK arts nonprofit with this engagement profile; no comparable data in the deliverable.
- Whether the member benefits (priority booking, two events a year, partner discount) are actually valuable to the audience.
- Whether the soft-launch audience—"the 200 most engaged contacts"—can be reliably identified and whether their conversion rate will predict the full list.
- The operational reality of member-only events on current staff capacity; depends on scheduling, existing event format and size.

QUESTIONS WORTH ASKING THE TEAM

1. If a soft launch to your most-engaged 200 hits 3%, do you launch anyway? The answer tells you if 5% is the real gate or a placeholder.
2. What did you learn from the comparable organizations—not just their price point, but their first-year conversion and what it took to achieve it?
3. Does "the 200 most engaged" come from attendance records, email engagement, donation history, or something else? How confident are you in that measure?
4. What is the plan if month two shows that member-only events are not feasible on the current schedule—do you redesign the offer or pause growth?
