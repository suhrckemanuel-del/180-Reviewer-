WHAT WORKS

The decision frame is clear and bounded. "Client E should launch a paid membership scheme, starting with a single tier in the autumn" (Slide 1) and the reasoning on Slide 2 moves straight to the answer and its constraint. This is how the client needs to read it.

The soft-launch gate is pragmatic. Slide 10's three-month timeline with a 5% conversion review threshold before full launch is honest risk management on a small nonprofit's budget and gives the client a real decision point, not a binary bet.

WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER

1. The conversion assumption is stated as conservative but the reasoning is not shown. [High impact]
   Where: Slide 8
   Quote: "The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test."
   Why it costs the client: The board is being asked to act on this 8% figure in Slide 10's timeline. "Main thing to test" names it as uncertain, which is honest, but the connection between 32% attendance and 8% conversion membership is never explained. The client cannot evaluate whether 8% is conservative or optimistic without that reasoning. They cannot defend it to their board either.
   What to do: Show the logic. How does a 32% event-attendance rate translate to 8% membership conversion? What is the assumed drop-off from "sees the soft launch" to "converts"? Are there benchmarks from the comparable organizations' membership? If the 8% is intuition, say so and show what assumptions would move it up or down.
   Answer first: Why should a 32% attendance rate imply that 8% of the mailing list will join? What does the team think the gap is, and why?

2. The comparable pricing is sourced to websites but the source list is not traceable. [Moderate]
   Where: Slide 5
   Quote: "Three comparable local arts non-profits run membership at 40 to 60 pounds a year (their public websites, 2025)."
   Why it costs the client: You can verify this in seconds if you have the URLs. Without them, the board cannot. This is not an accusation of error—it is a sourcing requirement for a client presentation. The decision on price point hinges on whether the 40–60 range is accurate, and "public websites" is where you found it, but which ones?
   What to do: Before the client meeting, add a footnote or appendix that names the three organizations and their current membership prices. One sentence, three URLs or organization names. It closes a gap without changing the deck.
   Answer first: Can the team hand back the list of comparable organizations and their current pricing in writing, verified today?

3. The implementation plan names responsibility but gives no starting action. [High impact]
   Where: Slide 10
   Quote: "The Client E coordinator owns the launch. Month one: soft launch to 200 contacts."
   Why it costs the client: "Soft launch to 200 contacts" is a destination, not a first step. The coordinator needs to know Monday morning what to do: send an email, build a form, compile a list of the 200 names, design the member benefits page, something concrete. "Month one: soft launch" leaves the hard work on their side of the table and costs them the hours the team could have saved by working it through once.
   What to do: Draft the first action. It does not need to be polished. One sample outreach email to those 200 contacts, or a list of the data fields needed to segment them, or a one-page project charter the coordinator can hand to their board. The principle is: if the team had to hand this to the coordinator today, what would be the smallest thing they would need to not start from zero?
   Answer first: What is the single first task the coordinator does in week one, and what would they need in hand to do it?

4. "Members-only events do not strain staff" is assumed but the capacity is not verified. [Moderate]
   Where: Slide 9
   Quote: "We assume the mailing list is accurate and that members-only events do not strain staff."
   Why it costs the client: You named this as an assumption, which is good. But the constraint block says "One part-time coordinator. No budget for new staff." Two members-only events a year is not huge, but the risk section does not show that you have tested whether two extra events actually fit the coordinator's hours. If the soft launch goes to 5% conversion, that is 70 members. Are two events a year for 70 people something a part-time coordinator already running the main program can absorb? The risk is named but not pressure-tested.
   What to do: Test the capacity claim. How many hours do two member events take? What is the coordinator currently running? Is there a month when they have the bandwidth and one where they do not? If the answer is "yes, they can," show the work. If it is "we don't know," that is the honest risk to name, not an assumption to move past.
   Answer first: Based on the coordinator's current calendar or workload, can two members-only events a year be run without new hire or budget?

5. The revenue projection does not surface the conversion path clearly. [Moderate]
   Where: Slide 8
   Quote: "If 8% of the mailing list joins at 45 pounds, that is about 5,000 pounds in year one."
   Why it costs the client: This math is right (1,400 × 0.08 × 45 ≈ 5,040). But the deck never says: of the 1,400 on the list, how many will actually see the soft launch outreach, and are there other drops? The 1,400 is a mailing list. Do all 1,400 open emails? Is the soft launch only to 200 (Slide 10), meaning the 8% is 8% of all 1,400 or 8% of the 200? The arithmetic is transparent but the denominator has shifted without notice.
   What to do: Clarify the conversion path in writing. "Month one: soft launch to 200 of the 1,400 most engaged. Target: 5% (= 10 members) in the test. Month three: if achieved, full launch to the 1,400, targeting 8% (= 112 members)." This is clearer and shows whether the 8% is a global target or a best-case for the full launch.
   Answer first: Is the 8% conversion rate the expectation for the 200 in the soft launch or for the full mailing list in month three?

THE HABIT TO BUILD

The weak habit: stating an assumption but not showing its pressure test. The pattern in this deck is to name risks and assumptions honestly (great) but then move on without showing the check. "We assume members-only events do not strain staff" — is that true? Show the coordinator's calendar. "8% assumption is conservative" — conservative against what logic? Show the math or the benchmark.

Reusable lesson: for every major assumption that drives the recommendation, ask: what would change my mind? If it is something you can check, check it. If it is something only the client can verify (like their own mailing list accuracy), say so clearly, not as an assumption to move past. The assumption block becomes a checklist, not a disclaimer.

Concrete exercise: take Slide 8 and Slide 9. For each number and assumption, write one sentence that would let the client verify or challenge it. "The 1,400 mailing list includes anyone who has given an email to Client E for any reason, last updated October 2024" (verifiable). "An 8% conversion is conservative based on a typical 5% for peer organizations plus Client E's 32% event attendance" (falsifiable, can be checked). Not every assumption needs a source, but the client needs to know what they are assuming and on what basis.

WHAT THIS REVIEW COULD NOT JUDGE

- Whether three local arts non-profits and 40–60 pounds pricing is the right comparison set (this requires domain knowledge of the sector)
- Whether a part-time coordinator with no budget support can truly run two member events without quality loss (depends on their calendar and priorities)
- Whether the soft-launch review gate of 5% is too strict or too lenient for a realistic pilot
- Whether "priority booking, two members-only events a year and a discount with local partners" will actually drive conversion at 8%
- What the actual current capacity of the coordinator is, hour by hour

QUESTIONS WORTH ASKING THE TEAM

1. Can you hand back the names and current membership prices of the three organizations you benchmarked against?
2. What does the coordinator's current weekly schedule look like, and how do two member events a year fit into it?
3. In month one, is the 8% target applied only to the 200 soft-launch contacts, or to the whole 1,400?
4. If the soft launch clears 5% but full launch would take capacity you don't have, how does the team expect month three to go?
5. How did you arrive at 8% specifically? What research or comparable program data supports this, beyond intuition from the 32% attendance rate?
