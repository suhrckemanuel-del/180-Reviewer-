WHAT WORKS

- The rationale for beginning with one tier is clear and linked to operational simplicity: “Launching one tier at 45 pounds, rather than three tiers at once, keeps admin low and lets Client E learn before it scales.” (Slide 6)
- The team is commendably transparent about the central uncertainty rather than presenting it as established fact: “The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test.” (Slide 8)

WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER

1. The 8% conversion assumption is not yet supported strongly enough to carry the projected income or the launch recommendation. [High impact]  
   Where: Slide 8  
   Quote: “The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test.”  
   Why it costs the client: Attendance and paid-membership conversion are different behaviours. The comparison does not show that 8% is conservative, so the board cannot tell whether the £5,000 projection is a realistic base case, an aspiration or merely a test scenario.  
   What to do: Present 8% explicitly as a planning hypothesis and show a small sensitivity range. Support the range with the closest available evidence, such as comparable organisations’ conversion data or Client E’s past response to paid appeals.  
   Answer first: What evidence connects event attendance or mailing-list engagement to willingness to pay £45 annually?

2. The pilot has two different success thresholds, leaving the scale-up decision unclear. [High impact]  
   Where: Slide 10  
   Quote: “Month two: review conversion against the 8% target. Month three: full launch if the soft launch clears 5%.”  
   Why it costs the client: The board cannot see why performance below the stated target would still justify a full launch. On a 200-person pilot, the difference is only six members, so the choice of threshold materially affects the decision.  
   What to do: Establish one decision framework before launch, including what happens below, near and above the expected range. Derive the thresholds from financial viability and delivery capacity rather than choosing percentages in isolation.  
   Answer first: What minimum number of members makes a wider launch worthwhile after administration and benefit-delivery costs?

3. The financial case shows gross membership income but not the net contribution or break-even point. [High impact]  
   Where: Slide 8  
   Quote: “If 8% of the mailing list joins at 45 pounds, that is about 5,000 pounds in year one.”  
   Why it costs the client: The £5,000 figure may overstate the amount available to diversify income because the deck does not deduct event, administration, payment-processing or partner-management costs. The board therefore cannot compare membership fairly with other income options.  
   What to do: Add a simple low/base/high model covering members, gross revenue, incremental costs, staff time and net contribution. Make clear which costs are estimated and which can genuinely be absorbed.  
   Answer first: After all incremental costs and coordinator time, how much unrestricted income would membership contribute?

4. The claim that the scheme fits existing capacity needs an operational estimate, particularly given the single part-time coordinator. [High impact]  
   Where: Slide 7  
   Quote: “These use capacity Client E already has.”  
   Why it costs the client: This assertion carries much of the feasibility case, but the deck does not estimate setup work, recurring administration, member enquiries, event delivery or partner coordination. The client could approve a financially modest scheme that creates a disproportionate workload.  
   What to do: Map the launch and recurring tasks, assign approximate hours and identify peak periods. Separate activities that reuse genuinely spare capacity from those that add work, then simplify the offer if it exceeds the coordinator’s available time.  
   Answer first: How many setup and recurring hours can the coordinator absorb without displacing existing delivery?

5. The pricing comparison provides a useful range, but it does not yet establish that £45 is appropriate for this particular offer. [Moderate]  
   Where: Slide 5  
   Quote: “Three comparable local arts non-profits run membership at 40 to 60 pounds a year (their public websites, 2025).”  
   Why it costs the client: Comparable prices show that £45 is plausible in the market, but not whether the organisations offer similar benefits, serve similar audiences or achieve healthy take-up. The board cannot yet distinguish a reasoned price from a midpoint chosen for convenience.  
   What to do: Compare the three offers on benefits, audience, scale and any visible evidence of membership traction. Pair that outside view with a lightweight willingness-to-pay test among Client E’s engaged contacts.  
   Answer first: In what relevant ways are the three organisations and their membership propositions genuinely comparable to Client E?

6. The risk assessment is too narrow for the board to understand the full downside of the recommendation. [Moderate]  
   Where: Slide 9  
   Quote: “The main risk is low conversion.”  
   Why it costs the client: Low conversion matters, but the proposal also depends on staff capacity, deliverable member benefits, partner participation and continued member value. Without showing those dependencies and mitigations together, the board cannot judge whether the soft launch tests the important risks.  
   What to do: Build a short risk register covering likelihood, consequence, early warning signs, mitigation and ownership. Keep it focused on risks created by the proposed scheme and state which ones the pilot can—and cannot—test.  
   Answer first: Apart from sign-up volume, what could make the scheme consume more time or money than it returns?

THE HABIT TO BUILD

Separate evidence, assumptions and decision rules. The weaker habit here is allowing a plausible number to move silently from an assumption into a forecast and then into a launch threshold. As an exercise, make a one-page chain for each recommendation: claim, supporting evidence, remaining assumption, test, success measure and resulting decision. Any broken link identifies the next piece of work.
