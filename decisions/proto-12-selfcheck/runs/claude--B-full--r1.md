WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER

1. The £5,000 year-one revenue projection has no context for whether it is material to the funding challenge. [High impact]
   Where: Slide 8, and Slide 3
   Quote: "If 8% of the mailing list joins at 45 pounds, that is about 5,000 pounds in year one." (Slide 8) and "Two of five grants are not expected to renew, per the funder letters on file." (Slide 3)
   Why it costs the client: The board cannot assess whether this membership scheme addresses the core problem or is a pilot-stage experiment. Replacing diversified income requires the client to judge the scale of the opportunity. Without knowing the value of the two at-risk grants, the board cannot decide whether a year-one revenue of £5,000 is enough to justify the effort and risk.
   What to do: Either state the value of the two grants at risk, or quantify the annual income gap the membership scheme is intended to help close. Then the board can see what fraction of the problem this scheme addresses. The recommendation does not change, but the board's realistic expectations for impact will.
   Answer first: What do the two at-risk grants represent in annual revenue?

2. The leap from 32% event attendance to 8% membership conversion is stated but not reasoned. [High impact]
   Where: Slide 4 and Slide 8
   Quote: "Client E has 1,400 people on its mailing list and a 32% event-attendance rate (box-office data, 2024)." (Slide 4) and "The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test." (Slide 8)
   Why it costs the client: The team's language ("conservative against") suggests the attendance rate supports the conversion assumption, but does not explain how. A board member might read this as evidence the 8% is grounded in behavior, when it is actually an unexplained jump. If the team's reasoning is sound (e.g., "frequent attendees show strong engagement and are therefore more likely to support the organisation"), that reasoning must be visible. If it is not sound (if event attendance does not predict membership willingness to pay), the board is being misled about the basis of the core assumption.
   What to do: Reason through the connection explicitly. Why would a 32% event attendance rate support or bound an 8% membership conversion rate? If the logic is "people who attend events are engaged and therefore more likely to pay," make that visible. If there is no connection, remove the comparison and stand on the soft launch as the real test of the assumption.
   Answer first: What evidence or reasoning connects event attendance to membership conversion likelihood?

3. The months-two decision is stated as a binary against the 8% target, but the gate condition is 5%. The deck does not state what happens if the soft launch converts at, for example, 6%. [Moderate]
   Where: Slide 10
   Quote: "Month two: review conversion against the 8% target. Month three: full launch if the soft launch clears 5%."
   Why it costs the client: The implementation includes two numbers: 8% (the full-year assumption) and 5% (the soft-launch gate). The board needs to know the decision rule: does 6% soft-launch conversion go to full launch or back to revision? The ambiguity could lead to the coordinator making a decision at month two without a clear framework. A clearer threshold removes discretion.
   What to do: State the decision rule at month two. Example: "If soft launch conversion exceeds 6%, proceed to full launch. If it falls below 5%, return to the team for redesign before attempting the full launch. If it lands between 5% and 6%, run a second small test before deciding." The numbers can vary; the point is that the coordinator has a clear rule.
   Answer first: What soft-launch conversion rate should trigger a go/no-go/pivot decision?

THE HABIT TO BUILD
Reversing unsound reasoning patterns: the team compared event attendance to membership conversion and stated the comparison without testing the logic first. Strong teams do the reverse: they identify the assumption (8% will convert) and then ask what would have to be true for that to hold (frequent attendees must care enough to pay). When that gap is not obvious, they either reason through it rigorously or lift the assumption to the test phase and stay silent on its likelihood. Your next deck: name the assumption clearly, trace the logical thread from evidence to assumption, and when that thread is invisible to you, do not state the assumption as if it has support.

WHAT THIS REVIEW COULD NOT JUDGE
- Whether the two at-risk grants are confirmed as at-risk by the funder letters on file, and their individual values.
- Whether the 1,400-person mailing list was built through recent engagement or includes dated names unlikely to respond.
- Whether Client E's existing events actually do have spare capacity to run members-only programming without strain.
- Whether the "most engaged 200 contacts" can be identified cleanly from the mailing list or require manual curation.
- Whether the pricing and benefits are tested, or whether the £45 tier and the specific perks are educated guesses.

QUESTIONS WORTH ASKING THE TEAM
1. What is the value of the two grants at risk, and what is the total annual income gap the board wants to address?
2. What is the source or reasoning for the 8% conversion assumption? Has the team run similar programs, interviewed prospects, or benchmarked similar non-profits' open membership conversions?
3. What metrics will you use to identify the "most engaged 200" for the soft launch, and can you verify that sample is representative of the full list?
4. If the soft launch converts at 6%, what is the decision: move to full launch, iterate on the offer, or gather more data?
5. Can the coordinator own the full soft launch and month-two review without additional support, and if so, how?
