WHAT WORKS

1. The recommendation is actionable and bounded. "Launch a single membership tier at 45 pounds in the autumn, starting with a soft launch to the 200 most engaged contacts." The team owns the decision and a clear path to test it.

2. Risk acknowledgment is honest. The deck names the main risk ("low conversion") and surfaces what is being assumed ("the mailing list is accurate and that members-only events do not strain staff"). This is the behaviour a board wants to see.

3. The problem framing is tight. "Project grants are 80% of Client E's income (2024 accounts) and each is single-year. Two of five grants are not expected to renew, per the funder letters on file." The team grounded the need in client data and named a specific deadline pressure.

WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER

1. The 8% conversion assumption is presented as conservative, but the comparator that would justify it is missing. [Moderate]
   Where: Slide 8
   Quote: "The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test."
   Why it costs the client: The team claims the assumption is conservative by comparing it to attendance rate, but attendance and conversion to paid membership are different behaviors. A board reviewing this will ask what conversion rate the team is banking on the soft launch hitting, and "more conservative than attendance" does not answer that. If the assumption fails, the board needs to know whether the team expected 8% based on any comparable data or was guessing. The client cannot distinguish.
   What to do: Find or build a comparator for the 8% figure. Either cite a comparable membership program's conversion rate (if one exists in your research), or state what you are comparing 8% against if the 32% attendance figure is not the right one. If you have no comparable data, say so and name what the soft launch is designed to establish. The claim "conservative" has to survive because you can point to what it is conservative against.
   Answer first: What data or reasoning would justify calling 8% conservative rather than cautious?

2. The soft launch success criteria are stated in month-two terms but not embedded in the recommendation itself. [Moderate]
   Where: Slide 10
   Quote: "Month two: review conversion against the 8% target. Month three: full launch if the soft launch clears 5%."
   Why it costs the client: The board is seeing a 5% threshold for the first time here, after you have already built the case on 8%. The team needs to explain why 5% is the right gate (why not 3%, why not 7%?) and when that judgment was made. A board reading this may assume 5% is a fallback because the team is not confident in 8%, or they may read it as conservative and want to be more aggressive. The recommendation appears incomplete without that bridge.
   What to do: State the soft-launch success threshold in the main recommendation or its supporting slide, with the reasoning for why that number gates the full launch. Example: "We will launch fully only if the soft launch clears 5%, which we take as a sign that the core offer is viable even with only our most engaged 200 contacts." Keep it brief, but make the decision visible alongside the threshold.
   Answer first: Why does a 5% soft-launch conversion rate justify moving to a full launch if the main case is built on 8%?

3. The comparable pricing research is limited in scope and does not address whether the model is defensible. [Moderate]
   Where: Slide 5
   Quote: "Three comparable local arts non-profits run membership at 40 to 60 pounds a year (their public websites, 2025)."
   Why it costs the client: The team found three local groups and took the middle of their range. That is market research, but it is not analysis. The board will want to know whether those groups are true comparables (what is their scale, their event frequency, the member benefits?) and whether 45 pounds is defensible given Client E's specific capacity and positioning. A board could approve based on "the market says 40-60" and still fail if Client E's offering does not justify that price point. The analysis has to answer whether this price is right for this client, not just whether it is in the market range.
   What to do: Name what makes the three groups comparable to Client E (size, event frequency, member base size, benefit set). If they are not closely comparable, say so and state what the team is using them to calibrate. Then address what justifies 45 pounds for Client E specifically: is it the value of the benefits (two members-only events), the price sensitivity of the engaged base, or something else? One or two sentences, but the chain has to close: comparable groups set a range, and here is why Client E fits this point in it.
   Answer first: Are the three comparable groups actually similar enough to Client E that their pricing should govern this decision?

WHAT THIS REVIEW COULD NOT JUDGE

- Whether the "32% event-attendance rate" on a mailing list of 1,400 is a reliable proxy for membership conversion. It depends on whether attendance is a signal of engagement or of baseline interest, which requires knowledge of how Client E's audience behaves.
- Whether Client E's coordinator can absorb the soft launch administration (200 contacts, conversion tracking, member event setup) without reducing other work, or whether it will require spillover.
- Whether local arts non-profit membership in this region has experienced recent changes (cost-of-living effects, membership saturation, new competitors) that would affect the model's viability. The 2025 pricing comparison is fresh but does not address whether demand has shifted.
- Whether the "members-only events" and "discount with local partners" are attractive enough to move a 32% attendee to a paying member, or whether they are threshold costs the team is assuming the audience will accept.

QUESTIONS WORTH ASKING THE TEAM

1. What is the soft launch designed to test beyond the 5% threshold? That is, what specific finding would make you advise the client not to proceed with the full launch, even if you hit the 5% bar?
2. If the soft launch clears 5% but falls short of 8%, what would the client do? Is that a success to build on or a signal to pause?
3. Have you modeled what happens to the coordinator's capacity in months two and three if the soft launch is hitting target? Is the full-launch workload scoped?
4. The members-only events are assumed not to "strain staff" — what is the current utilisation of the coordinator in that month, and how many additional hours would two events require?
5. If the 8% assumption is right, Client E would have roughly 112 paying members by month three. How would the team recommend segmenting or scaling the offering after that point?
