WHAT WORKS

- The income-risk case is concrete and sourced to client records. Slide 3: "Project grants are 80% of Client E's income (2024 accounts) and each is single-year."

WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER

1. The 8% conversion assumption is named honestly but not yet made decision-grade. [High impact]
   Where: Slide 8
   Quote: "If 8% of the mailing list joins at 45 pounds, that is about 5,000 pounds in year one."
   Why it costs the client: The board may approve the scheme expecting a roughly 5,000 pound upside without knowing whether 8% is a realistic paid-membership conversion rate, especially because attendance and payment are different behaviours.
   What to do: Show the assumption bridge. Either benchmark 8% against comparable membership conversion evidence, derive it from Client E behaviour or frame the 5,000 pounds as a test scenario rather than a forecast.
   Answer first: What evidence would make the board believe 8% is a reasonable paid-conversion assumption rather than a hopeful planning number?

2. The deck compares 8% conversion to 32% attendance but does not say why that comparator is valid. [Moderate]
   Where: Slide 8
   Quote: "The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test."
   Why it costs the client: A high attendance rate may show engagement, but it does not automatically show willingness to pay annually. The client needs to know whether attendance is a meaningful predictor of membership uptake.
   What to do: Explain the causal link or replace the comparator. Use attendance only if you can show why event attenders are likely joiners, otherwise compare 8% to a more relevant conversion rate or to the soft-launch hurdle.
   Answer first: Compared to what should the board judge 8%: prior donor behaviour, event repeat attendance, peer membership uptake or the minimum viable launch target?

3. The capacity risk is acknowledged but not tested enough for a one-coordinator organisation. [Moderate]
   Where: Slide 9
   Quote: "We assume the mailing list is accurate and that members-only events do not strain staff."
   Why it costs the client: The scheme could be financially sensible and still fail operationally if the promised benefits create work the part-time coordinator cannot absorb.
   What to do: Convert the assumption into a launch gate. Size the main workload items, define what “does not strain staff” means and make the soft launch test admin burden as well as conversion.
   Answer first: How many coordinator hours does the soft launch require and what workload threshold would stop or simplify the full launch?

4. The recommendation appears before the reason in a single governing sentence, so the fast reader gets the answer but not the logic. [Minor]
   Where: Slide 1
   Quote: "Client E should launch a paid membership scheme, starting with a single tier in the autumn."
   Why it costs the client: A hurried board reader sees the recommendation, but has to assemble the reason from later slides before understanding why this is the right size and timing.
   What to do: Add one early governing insight that combines the action and the reason. Keep it as a decision sentence, not an agenda or summary of topics.
   Answer first: What is the single reason the board should choose a small autumn launch rather than no launch or a larger launch?

THE HABIT TO BUILD
Build the habit of turning every critical assumption into a decision test. The weak habit here is stating a reasonable assumption and then moving on. Exercise: for each number the recommendation depends on, write “compared to what, so what, and what would change our advice?” before drafting the slide.
