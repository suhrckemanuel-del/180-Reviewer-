WHAT WORKS

The recommendation is explicit and feasible. Slide 1 names the decision without hedging: launch a single tier at 45 pounds in autumn. The team avoids the temptation to offer multiple options and instead commits to a bounded path. That is the work of a consultant, not a research report. The client knows what to do next.

The staged launch and test framework is well-constructed. Soft launch to 200 of the most engaged contacts (Slide 10) is operationally sound for a part-time-staffed organization. It trades scale for learning, which is the right trade for a team with one coordinator. The explicit timeline (month one to month three) gives Client E a calendar to work from.

WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER

1. The 8% conversion figure is the single decision-driver and it carries no evidence or comparator. [High impact]
   Where: Slide 8
   Quote: "If 8% of the mailing list joins at 45 pounds, that is about 5,000 pounds in year one. The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test."
   Why it costs the client: The client is being asked to commit to a launch path based on revenue math that rests on a number with no foundation. Saying the number is "conservative" relative to attendance does not explain it. Event attendance and membership conversion are different behaviors; many people will attend a free event who will not pay to join. The client needs to know: did you find this number from comparable schemes, did you reason it from Client E's data, or is this an educated guess? A 5,000 pound year-one revenue changes what Client E can do; the assumption deserves to be shown.
   What to do: Research what comparable membership schemes in the UK arts sector report for conversion on cold audiences (cold list) versus engaged audiences (people who have already attended). Name the source: "Research on X similar schemes suggests conversion of Y% to Z%." Then state how you landed on 8%: "This is Z% minus a margin for Client E's lower visibility." That transparency is more useful than apparent confidence. If you do not have source data, say so and call it a hypothesis the soft launch will test.
   Answer first: From what sources did you estimate 8% as the conversion rate, and what would make you move it up or down?

2. The membership benefits are not connected to what the audience actually values. [Moderate]
   Where: Slide 7
   Quote: "Priority booking, two members-only events a year and a discount with local partners. These use capacity Client E already has."
   Why it costs the client: The team designed benefits to minimize workload, which is pragmatic. The client needs to know whether these benefits will actually motivate a mailing-list member to pay 45 pounds. Someone who attends 32% of events might already get decent scheduling; someone might never want members-only events; someone might only care about the discount. Designing for capacity is smart; ignoring what the audience wants is the consulting-value failure. The soft launch should test this, but the deck should show that the team has thought about whether these benefits match the audience.
   What to do: State what you know about the audience: "Client E's event attendees tend to X [come back repeatedly / ask about accessibility / bring friends]. Based on that, we chose benefits that Y." Or: "We designed these benefits for operational simplicity and the soft launch will show whether they actually drive conversions. If not, we will pivot." Either way, show that the benefits were a deliberate choice, not just whatever fit the capacity constraint.
   Answer first: What do you know about what Client E's audience values, and how did that shape the benefits you chose?

3. The risk section misses the operational reality of a one-person launch. [Moderate]
   Where: Slide 9
   Quote: "The main risk is low conversion. We assume the mailing list is accurate and that members-only events do not strain staff."
   Why it costs the client: Two big risks are named, then assumed away: "We assume...members-only events do not strain staff." For a one-person operation, this is not an assumption, it is a hope. Slide 10 places all ownership on the coordinator. If the soft launch is successful, the workload grows in month three for full launch. The deck does not show how the coordinator manages both the soft launch ramp and the normal programming at the same time. There is also no contingency: what if conversion is lower than the 5% floor? Does the idea stop, or does the team pivot and try again?
   What to do: Add a sentence about how the coordinator will actually carry both the soft launch and normal work in month one. If new hours are needed, name them and show that Client E knows. Then name what happens if soft launch results disappoint: does the team try again? Pause? Pivot to a different model? A decision rule here protects both Client E and the team.
   Answer first: In reality, how many hours will the soft launch add to the coordinator's month-one workload, and where will those hours come from?

4. Outside view is missing. The deck does not show what Client E should learn from the broader market. [Moderate]
   Where: Slides 4, 5, 6
   Quote: "Client E has 1,400 people on its mailing list and a 32% event-attendance rate (box-office data, 2024). This is an engaged base to convert." / "Three comparable local arts non-profits run membership at 40 to 60 pounds a year (their public websites, 2025)." / "Launching one tier at 45 pounds, rather than three tiers at once, keeps admin low and lets Client E learn before it scales."
   Why it costs the client: The team looked at price (good) but did not report on market context. Are membership schemes growing or dying in the UK arts sector? Do small non-profits with Client E's budget actually manage memberships successfully, or is this a strategy that works only for larger organizations? Are there membership models that have failed in this space that Client E should avoid? The deck needs at least one sentence from the broader market view so Client E knows it is not walking into a known failing pattern.
   What to do: Add one finding about the market. Example: "UK arts non-profit membership has grown 12% year-over-year since 2020, with average retention at X%. Schemes under 500 members typically require Y staff hours. Client E's 1,400-list base is above average for this sector." This gives Client E confidence that they are entering a live market, not a saturated or dead one.
   Answer first: What does the broader market tell you about whether membership is a good strategy right now for small UK arts non-profits?

THE HABIT TO BUILD

The habit is the distance between "we checked this" and "here is what the check means." You researched the income problem, you looked at comparable prices and audiences, and you designed a test. The next step is always to tell the client what you learned and what it implies. When you say the 32% attendance rate means there is an engaged base, show the client the gap you are crossing: what happens to someone who attends 32% of events when they are asked to pay to join? That is the question. Show the data or the reasoning that makes you believe conversion is possible, not just plausible.

The exercise: take each evidence piece in the deck (the three comparables, the mailing list size, the attendance rate) and write one sentence that says what Client E should conclude from it. If you cannot write that sentence, that evidence is not doing its job yet.

WHAT THIS REVIEW COULD NOT JUDGE

- Whether 8% is a realistic conversion assumption for a paid membership in the arts non-profit sector given the price point and benefits.
- Whether Client E's 1,400-person mailing list is actually engaged or includes significant stale or disengaged addresses.
- Whether the identity and quality of the local partner organizations offering discounts are sufficient to make that benefit valuable to members.
- Whether Client E's autumn calendar is actually the best launch window or whether a different season would reduce coordinator workload and increase engagement.

QUESTIONS WORTH ASKING THE TEAM

1. You said 8% is conservative against the 32% attendance rate. Walk me through that math: what is the actual reasoning that connects event attendance to membership conversion?
2. Do you know whether the three comparable schemes you researched are growing their membership base or shrinking it? If one of them is, what is that scheme doing differently?
3. Have you tested the mailing list for deliverability recently, or are you assuming addresses from the 2024 box-office data are all still good?
4. If the soft launch reaches 6%, is that a go or a pause? You said launch if it clears 5%, but what is the actual decision rule between 5% and 8%?
5. The coordinator owns the launch in month one. Does Client E have a backup plan if the coordinator is unavailable or if the workload is larger than expected?
