WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER
COUNT: 0 high impact, 3 moderate, 0 minor

1. Nobody can tell whether 8% is conservative. [Moderate]
   Slide 8 - “The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test.”
   The problem: Event attendance is not a membership-conversion benchmark, so the deck does not establish whether 8% is cautious, ambitious or plausible.
   Why it matters: The board is asked to approve a membership launch against a stated income case. Comparing attendance with conversion may make the target appear safer than it is, changing whether the board approves the pilot and what it expects from it.
   Do this: Find a relevant conversion comparator, such as Client E's past paid-offer response or comparable membership conversion data. State what that comparison implies for the income case and the pilot target.
   Aim for a line like: If a comparable or representative test converts at 3%, projected membership income falls far below the 8% case, so the board should revisit the offer before a full launch.
   Answer first: What evidence shows the conversion rate a comparable membership offer can realistically achieve?

2. The soft launch cannot by itself justify a full launch. [Moderate]
   Slide 9 - “A soft launch to the most engaged 200 contacts tests both before the full launch.”
   Slide 10 - “Month three: full launch if the soft launch clears 5%.”
   The problem: The pilot reaches the most engaged contacts, but the deck does not show that their response represents the wider mailing list or that 5% is the right threshold for a full launch.
   Why it matters: The board could scale an offer on the basis of unusually favourable early demand, while the wider list responds differently. That changes whether full launch is justified after month two rather than whether the pilot itself proceeds.
   Do this: Define what the pilot can and cannot validate, then set the scale decision against representative demand, expected income and coordinator capacity. Explain how the full-launch decision changes if the pilot result does not generalise.
   Aim for a line like: If the most engaged contacts convert at 8% but a broader test group converts at 3%, Client E should reassess full-launch economics before scaling.
   Answer first: What result from a high-engagement group would be sufficient evidence that the full mailing list will convert?

3. The launch case is stated before its governing reason. [Moderate]
   Slide 1 - “Client E should launch a paid membership scheme, starting with a single tier in the autumn.”
   Slide 2 - “Our answer is yes, with a deliberately small start.”
   The problem: Neither opening sentence explains why the board should choose this route, so the decision, income-risk rationale and evidence appear separately across the deck.
   Why it matters: A board member reading quickly can see the requested action but not the basis for choosing it. That can turn approval into a vague endorsement of membership rather than approval of a controlled response to grant risk.
   Do this: Put one governing insight near the start that joins the recommended action with the evidence-based reason for it. Use the rest of the deck to prove that single argument.
   Answer first: What is the one reason the board should approve a single-tier pilot now rather than retain the current income model?
