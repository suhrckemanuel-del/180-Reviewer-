ON YOUR OWN READ
You were right that the coordinator's concrete next step is missing. The implementation plan tells the lead what will happen, not what the coordinator will do on day one. You were also right to question the 8% figure—the deck describes it as conservative but never shows the work. That said, your worry about sourcing is partly accurate (the 8% is unsourced) and partly harder to spot than you thought: the 1,400 mailing list and 32% attendance both come from the same source, which is fine, but nobody will know that without asking the team. The omission that matters is the conversion benchmark, not the mailing list.

WHAT WORKS
1. The implementation plan is concrete and staged. "Month one: soft launch to 200 contacts. Month two: review conversion against the 8% target. Month three: full launch if the soft launch clears 5%." (Slide 10) This gives the coordinator a runway and a gate, which is exactly what a non-profit with one person can execute.

2. The risk framing is honest. "The main risk is low conversion. We assume the mailing list is accurate and that members-only events do not strain staff." (Slide 9) The team names what could fail and what they are betting on.

WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER
COUNT: 2 high impact, 2 moderate

1. Nobody can tell whether 8% is defensible. [High impact]
   Slide 8 - "If 8% of the mailing list joins at 45 pounds, that is about 5,000 pounds in year one. The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test."
   The problem: You claim the assumption is conservative but never say what it is conservative against. Against what figure? The comparison is incomplete and the reader cannot judge risk.
   Why it matters: The board will see this number—5,000 pounds—and will wonder whether it is plausible. To answer that question, they need to know what benchmark makes 8% conservative. They do not have it. If the board disagrees with the assumption, they cannot offer a figure in reply because you have not named the baseline. You lose the chance to land early agreement on what counts as success.
   Do this: Name the source or comparison the 8% rests on. If it comes from industry data (e.g. "conversion is typically 3-5% for cold outreach"), say so. If it comes from the 32% attendance rate (your current text suggests this), show the math: "If 32% of our list attends an event, X% of attendees typically stay for a transactional interaction like a membership pitch, suggesting Y% of the full list would convert."
   Aim for a line like: If typical membership conversion for engaged attendees runs 10-15%, then 8% of the whole list (where a third are active attendees) is materially conservative.
   Answer first: Where does an 8% conversion rate come from, and what sets it apart from the risk?

2. "Conservative" overstates confidence in the assumption. [High impact]
   Slide 8 - "The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test."
   The problem: You describe the assumption as conservative but acknowledge immediately after that it is the main thing to test. If you are testing it, you do not yet know whether it is conservative or aggressive. The word choice plants false confidence.
   Why it matters: A board reading this will hear "we have cushion in our numbers" when what the team actually means is "we are unsure." The mismatch between the language and the plan to test creates the wrong expectation. When the board sees the monthly review hits 4% in the soft launch and not 5%, they will suspect the team lowballed the projection rather than that the assumption was wrong.
   Do this: Drop "conservative." Say "The 8% assumption is unproven and the main thing to test." If you want to keep one claim, state the specific direction of uncertainty: "If attendance and membership conversion track historical arts-non-profit rates, 8% should be achievable. We will verify in the soft launch."
   Aim for a line like: If published membership schemes in similar organizations run 10-15% conversion among active attendees, and we reach 32% of our list with active engagement, then 8% of our full list is anchored conservatively.
   Answer first: What published data exists on conversion for membership schemes in community arts non-profits?

3. The board will not know what the coordinator does on week one. [Moderate]
   Slide 10 - "The Client E coordinator owns the launch. Month one: soft launch to 200 contacts. Month two: review conversion against the 8% target. Month three: full launch if the soft launch clears 5%."
   The problem: This slide tells the board the timeline, not what the coordinator will actually do. Select 200 people how? Design an email how? Offer what to sign up for? The board will ask "Is this actually ready to go?" and the deck does not give them the grounds to believe it is.
   Why it matters: The board is not signing off on a plan. They are signing off on whether the coordinator can pull it off with the support the organization can give. The client has already said there is no budget for new staff and one person will run this. You have not shown the board that the soft launch is actually doable, only that the timeline is staged.
   Do this: Draft one concrete action the coordinator takes in week one. Not full text—a direction. Example: "You will reach out to the top-engagement 200 from the mailing list (segmented by recent attendance). You will draft a short email pitch for review with the director, naming the offer (priority booking plus discount) and asking for a soft commitment. You will send that email at <date> and track opens and clicks." This does not rewrite the slide, and it lets you name what could go wrong in week one.
   Answer first: What is the first specific action the coordinator will take to launch the soft campaign, and what tool or data will they use to do it?

4. The mailing list and attendance numbers sit together without clear sourcing. [Moderate]
   Slide 4 - "Client E has 1,400 people on its mailing list and a 32% event-attendance rate (box-office data, 2024)."
   The problem: This slide cites "box-office data" but does not clarify whether both numbers come from the same place, whether 32% means 32% of the 1,400, or whether 1,400 is the mailing list outside of attendees. A reader cannot tell.
   Why it matters: If 1,400 is the mailing list and 32% is the attendance rate (so ~450 people attend events), then the addressable base for membership is narrower than it seems. If the 32% is the proportion of mailing-list members who are also active attendees, the 8% conversion sits on a smaller proven group. The board will compute different risks depending on how they read these numbers, and you are not controlling for that.
   Do this: Clarify in one phrase: "1,400 people on its mailing list, of which 32% attended at least one event in 2024 (so ~450 active attendees per box-office data)."
   Answer first: Of the 1,400 people, how many attended at least one event in 2024?

WHAT THIS REVIEW COULD NOT JUDGE
- Whether the board's own sense of membership viability matches the team's. You would need to know what the board thinks is realistic.
- Whether the soft-launch gate (5% conversion) is actually achievable given the lead time and the coordinator's other work. The team knows better than I do.
- Whether the mailing list itself is current. You note this is an assumption. If it is six months old, the addressable base is smaller.

QUESTIONS WORTH ASKING THE TEAM
1. What published membership conversion data for comparable arts non-profits exists, and does the team have access to it?
2. If the soft launch reaches only 4% conversion (below the 5% gate), what happens to the full launch timeline?
3. Does the coordinator have a tool or template to segment the 1,400 list by engagement level, or does that step get built during month one?
4. What does the director review the soft-launch email for? Sign-off, tone, or offer changes?
5. If the 8% assumption turns out to be aggressive and the board learns the first month cleared only 5%, how would the team explain that to the board, and what would you ask the board to decide next?
