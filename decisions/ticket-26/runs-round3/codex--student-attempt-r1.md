WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER
COUNT: 0 high impact, 3 moderate, 1 minor

1. Nobody knows which conversion result means launch. [Moderate]
   Slide 10 - "Month two: review conversion against the 8% target."
   Slide 10 - "Month three: full launch if the soft launch clears 5%."
   The problem: The deck sets 8% as the review target but uses 5% as the condition for full launch, without explaining how the thresholds work together.
   Why it matters: At a 6% soft-launch conversion, the board could scale because 5% is met or hold because 8% is missed. Those actions use the coordinator's limited capacity differently, and the deck gives the board no decision rule.
   Do this: Define the distinct purpose of each threshold, or use one clear go, hold and redesign rule. Tie that rule to the income case and capacity test.
   Answer first: What minimum conversion makes a full launch worthwhile after expected income and staff effort are considered?

2. Nobody can tell whether 8% is conservative. [Moderate]
   Slide 8 - "The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test."
   The problem: The forecast calls 8% conservative against a 32% attendance rate, but does not show that attendance predicts paid membership or whether about 5,000 pounds is material.
   Why it matters: The board may treat the forecast as meaningful diversification when it is too small for the income need or staff burden, or reject a viable low-cost scheme. Attendance and paid conversion answer different questions.
   Do this: Build a conversion range from a relevant membership benchmark or pilot result, then assess each scenario against the income contribution needed and coordinator hours. Label assumptions clearly.
   Aim for a line like: If a comparable membership conversion benchmark is 3%, an 8% launch case needs separate evidence before it can support the board's decision.
   Answer first: Which observed membership conversion rate, and which income contribution after staff effort, make the scheme worthwhile?

3. Existing capacity is claimed, not tested. [Moderate]
   Slide 7 - "These use capacity Client E already has."
   Slide 9 - "We assume the mailing list is accurate and that members-only events do not strain staff."
   The problem: The deck says the offer uses existing capacity, but it does not measure the coordinator's recurring work or define the capacity test for scaling.
   Why it matters: The board can approve income that displaces core programme work, while the coordinator has no additional support. A benefit available on paper is different from one that can be sustained after launch.
   Do this: Map the recurring work for payments, booking, queries and events, then set a capacity threshold beside the conversion threshold. Measure actual workload during the pilot before scaling.
   Aim for a line like: If pilot membership work takes 12 coordinator hours a month, the team can test whether that fits the capacity reserved for membership before scaling.
   Answer first: How many coordinator hours per month can Client E devote to membership without reducing committed activities?

4. The opening says what to do but not why in one pass. [Minor]
   Slide 1 - "Client E should launch a paid membership scheme, starting with a single tier in the autumn."
   Slide 2 - "Client E asked whether a paid membership could diversify income away from project grants."
   The problem: The opening gives the board an action but not one sentence linking that action to its decisive reason.
   Why it matters: A board reader sees “launch” before seeing the linked case for grant-risk diversification and cautious learning. The proposal can therefore read as a preference rather than a response to a defined income risk.
   Do this: Put the recommendation and its decisive reason together in the opening, then let later slides prove it. Preserve that this is a small test, not a fully validated income solution.
   Answer first: Which reason should the board treat as decisive for approving the pilot?
