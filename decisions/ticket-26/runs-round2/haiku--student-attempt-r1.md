ON YOUR OWN READ
You identified the core gap correctly: the recommendation tells the board what to do but stops short of showing the coordinator what that looks like in practice. The market diagnosis is genuinely strong, and the staged approach is sound. Where you lose the thread is at the step where action happens.

On the sourcing concern: the deck actually attributes most numbers to specific sources (accounts, box-office data, public websites). The 8% is stated as "our own assumption," which is honest. That leaves the board uncertain about the plausibility of the financial case, which is what your concern is pointing toward.

WHAT WORKS
The problem framing is specific and bounded: "Project grants are 80% of Client E's income (2024 accounts) and each is single-year. Two of five grants are not expected to renew" (slide 3). This gives the board a clear, real reason to act.

The client base is well-demonstrated: "Client E has 1,400 people on its mailing list and a 32% event-attendance rate (box-office data, 2024)" (slide 4) shows the audience exists and is engaged.

The staged approach respects the constraint: "Launching one tier at 45 pounds, rather than three tiers at once, keeps admin low and lets Client E learn before it scales" (slide 6), paired with the soft-launch gates, is realistic for one part-time coordinator.

WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER
COUNT: 0 high impact, 2 moderate, 1 minor

1. The implementation path is too abstract for the coordinator to act on immediately [Moderate]
   Slide 10 - "The Client E coordinator owns the launch. Month one: soft launch to 200 contacts."
   The problem: You name the owner and timeline, but not the mechanics. Soft launch to 200 contacts does not tell the coordinator which 200, how to reach them, what message to send, or what success looks like in week one.
   Why it matters: The board approves the recommendation. The coordinator reads it and then is stuck on the step where it becomes real work. The recommendation is abstract at the precise point where the client has to commit resources and the coordinator has to know what to do.
   Do this: Write the actual contact strategy. Which 200 people (sorted by attendance frequency, event type, or engagement score)? How does the coordinator reach them (email, postal mail, text, phone)? What goes in the opening message and what offer is made (full price, trial pricing, exclusive event access)? What counts as success in the first 30 days (conversion rate, feedback collected, next decision point)?
   Aim for a line like: If the soft launch targets [the top 200 by event attendance in the past 18 months], reaches them by [email from the coordinator with a personalized line about an event they attended], and offers [two months at half price to test], the first week will show [whether price or trust is the barrier].
   Answer first: What is the coordinator literally sending, to whom, and what does a successful first month look like in numbers or feedback?

2. The 8% conversion assumption is unsourced and it is what the board is uncertain about [Moderate]
   Slide 8 - "The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test."
   The problem: You state the assumption and position it as conservative relative to 32% event attendance. But you do not explain the bridge from one in three people attending an event to one in twelve buying membership. That jump needs reasoning before the board can set a real success threshold.
   Why it matters: Your own input says the team is unsure whether the 8% is defensible enough to put in front of the board. The assumption is the foundation of whether this is worth the coordinator's time. If the real conversion is 2%, the revenue drops to 1,250 pounds and the calculus changes. The board needs to know: is 8% based on any data, or is it a guess?
   Do this: Give the board one piece of evidence or reasoning for the 8%. This could be: a conversion rate from another membership program, the attendance distribution of the 1,400 (e.g., 300 people attend 5+ events a year and you will target them), or a simple model (e.g., if we contact the top 200 most-engaged people, one in six converts, that is 8% of the full list).
   Aim for a line like: If the 200 most-engaged attendees have a 15% conversion rate, and they make up half of the list likely to buy, then the soft launch will show [30 conversions].
   Answer first: What is the 8% based on, or what universe of people are actually likely to buy?

3. The 45-pound price lacks specific justification [Minor]
   Slide 5 to 6 - "Three comparable local arts non-profits run membership at 40 to 60 pounds a year... Launching one tier at 45 pounds, rather than three tiers at once."
   The problem: You cite the range and land on 45 but do not explain why. Is it the midpoint because you had no other signal, or does it reflect Client E's positioning or cost structure?
   Why it matters: The board sees a price and assumes there was reasoning. Without it, the choice looks arbitrary rather than strategic.
   Do this: Name the principle for 45 pounds. This could be: the midpoint of local comparables, signaling value for money; the price that your event-attendance numbers suggest willingness to pay; or the price that funds one member-only event per year at your current cost structure.
   Answer first: What specifically recommends 45 pounds for Client E, as opposed to 40 or 60?

WHAT THIS REVIEW COULD NOT JUDGE
Whether the 1,400 mailing list size is current and accurate (not sourced in the deliverable).
Whether Client E's cost structure can support the member-only events without straining staff beyond the stated constraints.
Whether the comparable organizations' membership programs are truly comparable in terms of size, sector positioning, or audience profile.

QUESTIONS WORTH ASKING THE TEAM
1. If the soft launch to 200 converts at 5% instead of 8%, does the recommendation change?
2. What does the coordinator do if they hit 8% conversion in the soft launch but the board is not confident the full launch will hold?
3. Have you road-tested the member benefits (priority booking, two members-only events) against what comparable organizations actually deliver, or is this designed from Client E's available capacity?
4. Will the coordinator track which attendees convert and which do not, and if so, what will you learn from the non-converters?
5. What is the smallest revenue number that would make the coordinator's time investment worth it?
