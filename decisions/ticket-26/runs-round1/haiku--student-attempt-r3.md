ON YOUR OWN READ
The student correctly identified that the implementation is too high-level (soft launch to 200 contacts, but not how) and that the recommendation stays abstract. Where their read missed: the real issue is not that membership numbers are pulled from one source, but that the 8% conversion assumption itself is unsourced and the logic connecting it to the 32% attendance rate is missing. The actual numbers (1,400 list size, 40-60 pound benchmarks) are all transparent; the assumption driving the financial case is not.

WHAT WORKS
The phased approach (soft launch, review, full launch) is smart risk management and lets Client E learn before scaling: "A soft launch to the most engaged 200 contacts tests both before the full launch" (Slide 9). The value prop uses capacity Client E already has, making the recommendation feasible within existing constraints: "Priority booking, two members-only events a year and a discount with local partners. These use capacity Client E already has." (Slide 7)

WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER
COUNT: 1 high impact, 2 moderate

1. The 8% conversion assumption is unsourced and its logic is missing [High impact]
   Slide 8 - "The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test."
   The problem: The board is asked to approve a financial case (5,000 pounds in year one) that rests on an unsourced number. "Conservative" is asserted but never explained. Why should a 32% event-attendance rate lead to an 8% membership conversion? That is a 4x drop with no derivation shown.
   Why it matters: The client cannot assess whether the financial case is sound or merely aspirational. The 32% attend events, but event attendance is not the same as willingness to pay for membership. Without seeing how the 8% was derived - from comparable organizations, email benchmarks, a pilot test, or domain knowledge - the board cannot evaluate whether this recommendation is grounded. The team flagged this as "the main thing to test," which signals they know it is the crux.
   Do this: Show the reasoning behind 8 percent. What do comparable non-profits see for email-to-member conversion? What did a quick sample of engaged contacts yield? Or reframe it as explicitly exploratory: state it as a hypothesis to test, not a conservative estimate. Specificity turns a vulnerability into strength.
   Answer first: Where does 8 percent come from, and what comparator would make it defensible?

2. The implementation is described in sequence but not in the detail needed to execute [Moderate]
   Slide 10 - "Month one: soft launch to 200 contacts."
   The problem: "Soft launch to 200 contacts" is an agenda item, not a plan. The coordinator owns the launch but is not told how to execute it. Via email? Direct mail? With what message? What is the call to action? How do people respond (link, phone, in person)? These decisions affect conversion directly.
   Why it matters: A plan without a worked first step is one the team has thought through but the coordinator has to carry out incomplete. Even small choices (email vs. letter vs. in-person) change the outcome. The soft launch is the test of the entire business case, so it has to be specific enough to run without guessing.
   Do this: Walk one contact end-to-end. Who is one of the 200? What message lands in their inbox or mailbox? What does the response look like (the URL they click, the form they fill, the confirmation they get)? One worked case beats a checklist.
   Answer first: What will the first 200 contacts actually receive, and how will they respond?

3. The risk assumptions are named but not validated [Moderate]
   Slide 9 - "We assume the mailing list is accurate and that members-only events do not strain staff."
   The problem: "Accurate" is not defined. Is the list 100 percent current, or 80 percent? If 20 percent is stale, does the 8 percent assumption still hold? The same for staff: did anyone test whether the coordinator can run two extra events a year without overload? Assumptions are stated but their validation is not.
   Why it matters: The board needs to know what could break this plan and how risky each break point is. If the list is stale, conversion tanks. If staff are stretched, the value prop (members-only events) becomes a cost burden, not a differentiator. Naming an assumption is a start; explaining what "good" looks like and how the team checked it is the finish.
   Do this: For each assumption, define what success looks like and what the soft launch will test. Example: the list is at least 85 percent current. You will know because soft launch response to the 200 engaged contacts will tell you their status. If you get under 3 percent response, the list needs cleaning before full launch. Metric beats narrative.
   Answer first: What makes each assumption true, and how will you know after the soft launch?

WHAT THIS REVIEW COULD NOT JUDGE
- Whether the proposed members-only events (priority booking, two events a year, local partner discount) are actually differentiated or valuable to this organization's contacts. That turns on what comparable organizations offer and what Client E's audience cares about.
- Whether a part-time coordinator has the bandwidth to run this alongside existing work, even with a small start. Staff capacity depends on the coordinator's current role and the client's definition of "manage."

QUESTIONS WORTH ASKING THE TEAM
1. Where does the 8 percent figure come from? Can you show a source - comparable organizations, email benchmarks, a quick pilot, or domain expertise - or reframe it explicitly as a hypothesis to test?
2. Run one member through the journey: walk us from mailing list to paid membership. What message, what channel, what response mechanism?
3. How did you validate that the coordinator has capacity to run the soft launch alongside existing work? What did they say?
4. What happens in month two if the soft launch converts at 4 percent instead of 8 percent? Does the recommendation change?
