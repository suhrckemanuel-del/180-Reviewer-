WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER
COUNT: 1 high impact, 1 moderate, 0 minor

1. The 8% conversion assumption carries the financial case and is unsourced. [High impact]
   Slide 8 - "If 8% of the mailing list joins at 45 pounds, that is about 5,000 pounds in year one. The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test."
   The problem: The deck calls 8% conversion "conservative" but does not name what it is conservative against. No source, no benchmark, no reasoning shown.
   Why it matters: The board will ask "where did 8% come from?" before they commit capital and staff time to a launch. Without that answer, the 5,000-pound forecast is an assertion, not a revenue projection. If the team has no data, it should not claim the assumption is conservative; it should call it a working hypothesis and concentrate all weight on the soft launch as the test.
   Do this: Search comparable nonprofits' websites and annual reports for membership conversion data. If you cannot find published data, drop the "conservative" claim and reframe this: "We assume 8% because [reason]. The soft launch will test whether it holds."
   Aim for a line like: "Comparable nonprofits report membership conversion of X-Y% from engaged audiences, making 8% conservative."
   Answer first: What membership conversion data can comparable nonprofits publish or will share with you?

2. The 32% attendance rate is used as evidence for the 8% conversion, but they measure different behaviors. [Moderate]
   Slide 8 - "The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate."
   The problem: Attendance and paid membership are not the same thing. The comparison makes the 8% look more grounded than it is.
   Why it matters: The board sees these two numbers side-by-side and thinks you have data showing conversion when you really have two unrelated metrics. It is not evidence; it is reasoning that skipped a step.
   Do this: Either find data on what fraction of event attendees become members at comparable organizations, or drop this comparison. Let the soft launch be your evidence, not backwards reasoning from attendance.
   Answer first: Do your comparable nonprofits report conversion from attendee to member, or is that data not available?

WHAT THIS REVIEW COULD NOT JUDGE
- Whether 200 contacts is a sample size large enough to distinguish 5% conversion from 8% with confidence you can rely on. That depends on Client E's contact retention and is not in the deck.
- Whether the comparable nonprofits' public websites show the full picture of their membership programs or whether sector benchmarks exist privately.

QUESTIONS WORTH ASKING THE TEAM
1. What membership conversion rates can you find from comparable nonprofits, or what internal baseline does Client E have from past engagement behavior?
2. If you have no conversion data to cite, what is your reasoning for 8%? What is the range of outcomes you expect?
3. In month two, how many actual conversions would you need to see in the 200-person soft launch before you feel confident moving to full launch?
4. Who at Client E owns the membership list and conversion tracking through month two and beyond? The coordinator is already part-time.
