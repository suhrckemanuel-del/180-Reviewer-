WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER
COUNT: 0 high impact, 3 moderate, 0 minor

1. Nobody can tell why 5% is enough when the case is built on 8% [Moderate]
   Slide 8 - "If 8% of the mailing list joins at 45 pounds, that is about 5,000 pounds in year one."
   Slide 10 - "Month three: full launch if the soft launch clears 5%."
   The problem: You build the £5,000 case at 8% but instruct full launch above 5%, without showing whether either result covers delivery costs or materially offsets lost grant income.
   Why it matters: The board is choosing whether the scheme is worth scarce coordinator capacity. A 5% result can be a sensible learning threshold yet still be economically too small, while an 8% result can look attractive before costs yet add little net diversification. Those contrasts determine whether the board launches, redesigns or declines the scheme.
   Do this: Build a 5%, 8% and 12% sensitivity showing revenue, direct costs, coordinator hours, net contribution and share of the non-renewing grant gap. Set one explicit go/no-go threshold from that economics and explain why it differs from the 8% target, if it does.
   Aim for a line like: At 5%, 8% and 12% conversion, £1,200 of delivery cost leaves £1,950, £3,840 and £6,360, equal to 7%, 14% and 23% of a £28,000 grant gap: that is the shape, not your numbers.
   Answer first: What minimum net contribution makes membership worth the coordinator's time relative to the grant gap?

2. The soft launch is biased toward the people most likely to join [Moderate]
   Slide 9 - "A soft launch to the most engaged 200 contacts tests both before the full launch."
   The problem: You treat response from the most engaged contacts as evidence for a full-list launch, but that selected group cannot estimate demand across the broader mailing list.
   Why it matters: The board's scale decision lands on a different population from the pilot. A 5% response among the most engaged contacts can coexist with a much lower response across the broader list, so the board could authorize full rollout on evidence that does not estimate full-rollout demand.
   Do this: Separate a low-risk operational rollout from a demand test. Use a random or stratified holdout, report conversion by engagement segment and require the full-launch rule to reflect expected conversion across the whole usable list.
   Answer first: Is the pilot meant to learn how to operate the offer or to estimate whole-list demand?

3. The capacity claim is an assumption, not a feasibility case [Moderate]
   Slide 7 - "These use capacity Client E already has."
   Slide 9 - "We assume the mailing list is accurate and that members-only events do not strain staff."
   The problem: You assert existing capacity and separately assume events will not strain staff, but do not estimate administration, event delivery, partner management or ongoing service hours.
   Why it matters: The board must trade net income against the only coordinator's time. An offer that fits financially can still displace programming or grant work, while a lighter benefit package may be feasible. That difference changes the offer, timing or decision to launch.
   Do this: Map tasks by launch and recurring phase, assign hours and identify what work is displaced. Stress-test the two events and partner discount process, then reduce benefits or defer launch if the workload exceeds an agreed capacity cap.
   Aim for a line like: At 16 launch hours and 3 recurring hours a month, membership uses 12% of the coordinator's available time: that is the shape, not your numbers.
   Answer first: How many coordinator hours can the board allocate without displacing core delivery or fundraising?
