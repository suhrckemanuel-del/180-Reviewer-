WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER

COUNT: 0 high impact, 3 moderate, 0 minor

1. The deck never gives the board one sentence that links the launch decision to its reason. [Moderate]  
   Slide 1 - “Client E should launch a paid membership scheme, starting with a single tier in the autumn.”  
   Slide 3 - “Project grants are 80% of Client E's income (2024 accounts) and each is single-year.”  
   The problem: The recommendation and its rationale appear separately, so the governing insight is absent from the reader’s first pass.  
   Why it matters: The board sees an instruction to launch before it sees why this is the right response to grant exposure. It must assemble the case itself from separate slides, instead of judging one explicit decision and its rationale.  
   Do this: Put the recommended action and the reason it addresses Client E’s income risk together in the opening decision statement. Make every later slide test or support that single claim.  
   Answer first: What specific income-risk outcome makes a membership launch preferable to the board’s alternatives?

2. The full-launch gate leaves the board unable to judge whether the launch is safe on existing capacity. [Moderate]  
   Slide 9 - “We assume the mailing list is accurate and that members-only events do not strain staff.”  
   Slide 10 - “Month two: review conversion against the 8% target. Month three: full launch if the soft launch clears 5%.”  
   The problem: The plan measures conversion, but its full-launch decision does not set tests for mailing-list quality or coordinator workload.  
   Why it matters: The board could approve a full launch at 5% conversion while the coordinator workload or event delivery is unsustainable. A successful demand test and a feasible operating model are different conditions for Client E.  
   Do this: Define go or no-go checks for staff time, delivery capacity and contact-data quality alongside conversion. State how the 5% launch gate relates to the 8% target.  
   Answer first: What workload and cost can the one part-time coordinator absorb before membership stops diversifying income?

3. Nobody can tell whether 8% is a defensible conversion assumption. [Moderate]  
   Slide 8 - “The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test.”  
   The problem: Attendance is not a membership-conversion comparator, so the deck does not show whether 8% is high, low or plausible.  
   Why it matters: The board may treat the year-one income estimate as a reliable basis for approving the scheme, while the only comparison measures event participation rather than willingness to pay. That can change the scale or timing of the launch it approves.  
   Do this: Test 8% against a relevant conversion benchmark, a prior Client E paid offer or a clearly bounded pilot result. Show what the comparison implies for the launch decision.  
   Answer first: What observed paid-conversion evidence would make 8% plausible for this audience?
