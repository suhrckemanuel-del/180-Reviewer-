WHAT TO FIX, IN ORDER
COUNT: 0 high impact, 2 moderate, 1 minor

1. Nobody can tell whether 5% or £5,000 is enough. [Moderate]  
   Slide 8: "If 8% of the mailing list joins at 45 pounds, that is about 5,000 pounds in year one."  
   Slide 8: "The 8% assumption is conservative against the 32% attendance rate and is the main thing to test."  
   Slide 10: "Month three: full launch if the soft launch clears 5%."  
   The problem: The deck gives an 8% projection and a 5% scale-up threshold but never compares either outcome with the income contribution Client E needs from membership. Attendance is not explained as a reliable conversion comparator.  
   Why it matters: The board must decide whether membership is worth using scarce coordinator capacity for. A 5% result could justify scaling if it materially diversifies income, or justify redesigning the offer if it does not. The deck currently gives no benchmark to distinguish those choices.  
   Do this: Agree the income contribution required to justify launch, then show the financial result at both 5% and 8%. Explain why attendance is a useful signal, and where it is not, for membership conversion.  
   Aim for a line like: If 4% of the mailing list joined, projected income would be £2,520, or [x]% of the income contribution required to justify launch: that is the shape, not your numbers.  
   Answer first: What annual income contribution would make the membership scheme worthwhile for Client E?

2. The pilot has no staff-capacity pass condition. [Moderate]  
   Slide 9: "We assume the mailing list is accurate and that members-only events do not strain staff."  
   Slide 9: "A soft launch to the most engaged 200 contacts tests both before the full launch."  
   Slide 10: "Month two: review conversion against the 8% target."  
   The problem: Slide 9 says the pilot tests staff strain, but the only stated review criterion is conversion. No workload measure or capacity threshold determines whether the coordinator can sustain a full launch.  
   Why it matters: The board could approve a full launch after a conversion pass even if membership administration displaces core work. The decision should distinguish a financially promising offer that fits existing capacity from one that requires the client to redesign delivery first.  
   Do this: Record the coordinator tasks and time during the pilot, then set a workload threshold alongside the conversion threshold. Make both conditions part of the scale-up decision.  
   Aim for a line like: If the first ten memberships require six coordinator hours each month, compare that workload with the capacity available before scaling: that is the shape, not your numbers.  
   Answer first: How many coordinator hours can Client E devote monthly without displacing core work?

3. The board must reconstruct why it should approve the launch. [Minor]  
   Slide 1: "Client E should launch a paid membership scheme, starting with a single tier in the autumn."  
   Slide 3: "Project grants are 80% of Client E's income (2024 accounts) and each is single-year."  
   The problem: No opening sentence joins the recommendation to its decision reason and test. The action appears first, while the income-risk rationale and financial case appear later.  
   Why it matters: A board reading quickly sees the proposed launch but must assemble the basis for approving it across later slides. That increases the chance that discussion moves straight to delivery details before the decision case is understood.  
   Do this: Open with one governing decision statement that joins the recommended launch, the income-risk rationale and the evidence threshold for scaling. Keep the later slides as support.  
   Answer first: What single result should make the board say yes to a small launch now?
